Finding Your Why for Lasting Change

You keep saying you just lack motivation to stay consistent. Coach Jonathan Hull explains why that's rarely the real problem, and shares a simple framework for finding your actual reason to change.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· September 3, 2026 · 7 min read
JH
Jonathan Hull
Health and wellness coach specializing in exercise, mindset, and lifestyle, with a background in exercise physiology.
Quick Answer

"I just lack motivation" is rarely the real problem. Resistance to a habit is often a logical response to not believing it will actually work in your life. Digging into what a goal would mean for yourself, your family, and your career, using the self, family, career framework, surfaces a far more useful reason to change than a surface-level goal alone.

Most people never dig past the surface of their own fitness goal. "I want to lose 10 pounds" feels like a complete reason on its own, when in reality, most people never ask themselves why that specific outcome actually matters. Coach Jonathan Hull breaks down a simple framework for finding that deeper reason, and why it matters more than raw motivation.

The Self, Family, Career Framework

Jonathan works through this framework with his clients directly: what would achieving this goal mean for you, for your family, and for your career? Then he asks the harder follow-up: what would it mean if you don't achieve it, for you, for your family, for your career?

It's not a fear factor, but it is a "let's get real about what we're trying to do here and what we're trying to accomplish."

This isn't about instilling fear, it's about getting specific and honest about the stakes involved, since a vague goal rarely sustains effort the way a genuinely understood reason does.

Why "I Just Lack Motivation" Is Often the Wrong Diagnosis

Jonathan shared an example from a client who said she was resistant to meal prepping and didn't know why, assuming she simply didn't have the time or desire. Digging deeper, they discovered the actual reason: she didn't believe meal prepping could realistically work within her specific life, not that she lacked motivation.

That's perfectly logical for you to resist doing that if you think there's no way for it to work for you.

Reframing the problem from "I lack motivation" to "I don't believe this specific approach fits my life" opens up a completely different, more productive conversation, one focused on finding an approach that actually could work, rather than trying to force more willpower onto something that was never going to fit.

Understanding Your Own Biases

Jonathan also emphasizes building self-awareness around your own cognitive biases, the quick, automatic thought patterns that help you make fast decisions but aren't always accurate. Knowing your typical blind spots, like assuming a plan won't work before genuinely trying it, is part of setting yourself up to actually follow through.

How to Apply This to Your Own Goal

1
Name your specific goal
Get concrete rather than vague, whether it's a number, a habit, or a specific capability.
2
Ask what it means for you, your family, and your career
Push past the surface answer to find the deeper personal stakes involved.
3
Ask the same question about not achieving it
This isn't about fear, it's about getting honest clarity on what's actually at stake.
4
Identify what's actually behind your resistance
If you're avoiding a specific habit, ask whether you genuinely believe it can work for your life, not just whether you "want" to do it.
Key Takeaways
  • The self, family, career framework helps surface a deeper, more specific reason behind a health goal.
  • Resistance to a specific habit is often a logical response to doubting it will work, not a lack of motivation.
  • Most people treat the reason behind their goal as self-evident, skipping deeper self-awareness entirely.
  • Reframing "I lack motivation" as "I don't believe this fits my life" opens up more productive problem-solving.
  • Understanding your own cognitive biases helps you recognize blind spots that quietly derail follow-through.

If you keep telling yourself you just lack motivation, it's worth asking a more specific question instead: do you actually believe the approach you're trying will work for your real life? That question, more than any pep talk, tends to reveal what's actually in the way.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the self, family, career framework for finding your why?

It means asking what achieving a specific health goal would mean for you personally, for your family, and for your career, and then asking the same question about what not achieving it would mean. This surfaces a deeper, more specific motivation than a surface-level goal alone.

Is resistance to healthy habits usually a lack of motivation?

Often not. Resistance to a specific habit, like meal prepping, is frequently a logical response to not believing the habit can realistically work in your life, rather than a simple lack of willpower or motivation.

Why do most people skip identifying the real reason behind their fitness goal?

Most people treat the reason behind a goal as self-evident, like assuming "I want to lose 10 pounds because I want to lose 10 pounds," without digging into the deeper, more specific motivation behind it.

What role do cognitive biases play in health and fitness decisions?

Cognitive biases are quick, automatic thought patterns that help you make fast decisions, but they aren't always accurate. Becoming aware of your own biases and blind spots is part of building the self-awareness needed to make lasting changes.
