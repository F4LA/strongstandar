Why You Keep Failing to Change Your Habits

You've tried hard, more than once, and it still didn't stick. Michael J. Lopez, former Ernst & Young and KPMG managing director turned change strategist, explains why that's almost never a discipline problem, and what actually is.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· July 27, 2026 · 9 min read
ML
Michael J. Lopez
Former managing director at Ernst & Young and KPMG, now a consultant and keynote speaker on organizational and personal change. Author of "Change," six science-backed strategies to transform your brain, body, and behavior.
Quick Answer

Habit change usually fails because of how the goal is built, not because of weak willpower. Two fixes matter most: write the goal as something to chase instead of something to avoid, and measure success by daily effort instead of a distant outcome. Expect friction around week three, since that's when your brain is unlearning an old pattern, not failing at a new one.

You've white-knuckled your way through a diet before. You've started the 5am workouts. You've deleted the apps, set the reminders, told your friends to hold you accountable. And a few weeks in, it fell apart anyway. So you've probably landed on the same conclusion most people do: you just don't have the discipline for this.

Michael J. Lopez has spent his career helping people and organizations change at the deepest level, and he says that conclusion is almost always wrong. People who struggle with change aren't lazy. Some of the people who struggle the most are the ones trying the hardest. What's actually missing is the design of the goal itself, and how your brain reacts to it.

Why the Goal You Set Is Already Working Against You

The Assumption Most People Make

When a habit doesn't stick, the instinct is to blame willpower. Try harder next time, find more discipline, want it more. Lopez says this instinct misses what's actually happening, and it keeps people re-running the exact same failed approach with more intensity instead of a different design.

What's Actually Happening in Your Brain

Lopez leans on the work of neuroscientist Dr. Lisa Feldman Barrett, who describes the brain as a prediction machine. Its job isn't to make you happy, it's to keep you alive, which means it's constantly scanning your past experience and guessing what comes next. If you've failed at a goal before, your brain's honest prediction the next time is that you'll fail again, and it will quietly work to confirm that guess.

That prediction machine also runs on a skewed input. Lopez cites research showing the average person says roughly seven negative things to themselves for every positive one. Layer an avoidance goal, something built entirely around the behavior you don't want, on top of a brain already wired toward negative prediction, and you've built a goal that fights you from two directions at once.

Avoidance Goal
  • "Stop eating out so much"
  • Focuses on the behavior you don't want
  • Keeps the unwanted behavior mentally front and center
  • Feels like restriction from day one
Approach Goal
  • "Cook three dinners at home this week"
  • Focuses on the behavior you want to build
  • Gives your brain something to chase
  • Feels like progress from day one

He puts it simply: the brain is at its best when it's chasing something, not avoiding it. If your goal is written as a thing to stop, you've built it to work against how your brain actually operates.

Why a Goal You're Handed Never Sticks Like One You Choose

There's a second piece Lopez points to that most people never examine: who the goal actually belongs to. His rule is simple. We own what we create. A goal someone else hands you, a spouse's suggestion, a doctor's recommendation, a generic New Year's resolution copied from somewhere, gets far less commitment than a goal you arrived at yourself. It's the same reason your own kids matter more to you than any other child on earth. You invested in them, so they became part of your identity.

This is also where Lopez brings in the second common mistake: defining the goal as a noun instead of a verb. A noun goal is an outcome, losing a certain amount of weight, hitting a certain number on the scale. The problem is you can't be successful until the exact moment that number arrives. If the distance between where you are and that number is large, the story your brain repeats every single day in between is: I am not succeeding. Even while you're moving in the right direction.

The fix is making the goal a verb, effort itself. Not "I am someone who accomplishes this outcome," but "I am someone who puts in effort toward this, today." Under that definition, going for a walk and doing bodyweight squats instead of skipping your workout entirely isn't a consolation prize. It's a win, because you showed up, on a goal that's actually yours.

Change is not a spectator sport. If you want to be different, at the end of the day, you have to do different.

Why It Feels Like You're Failing Even When You're Not

Here's the part that trips up people who are already doing everything right. Lopez is direct about it: progress is directional, not absolute. A 1% improvement on a hard day is just as real as a 10% improvement on an easy one, and nobody gets anywhere in a single step.

He tells clients to take the labels off a given day before judging it. What feels like a setback in the moment might be serving you in a way you can't see yet, because you don't get to know the second or third order effect of what you're doing right now. And the friction itself isn't a sign you're on the wrong track. Mistakes are, by a wide margin, the single greatest driver of neuroplasticity, the actual rewiring your brain has to do to make a new habit automatic. If you're not making mistakes, you're not in the process that builds the new pathway in the first place.

Why Quitter's Day Empties the Gyms Every Year

Quitter's Day, January 19th, is the day gyms across the country see their New Year's crowd disappear. It isn't a coincidence that it lands around three weeks in. Building something new as an adult requires two things happening at once: focusing on the new behavior, and actively unlearning or making space by removing an old one.

That process means making the mistakes described above, over and over. Most people don't enjoy making mistakes, so the moment the new habit collides with an old pathway, it feels like failure instead of a normal, necessary part of the process, and that's exactly the point where people quit.

How to Actually Build the New Habit

You don't need to fully believe you'll succeed before you start. Lopez describes two valid paths: some people need a belief that change is possible before they act, and some people build that belief through small wins that come from just getting started. He leans toward the second, and the research backs it up. Doing something you find hard and finishing it builds real, measurable confidence to do the next hard thing.

  • Make sure the goal is actually yours, not one you inherited from someone else
  • Rewrite it from what you'll stop doing to what you'll start doing
  • Replace the outcome with the daily effort as the actual target
  • Expect friction around week three, and treat it as necessary, not a failure
  • Let a small, finished action count as a real win, even if it's smaller than planned
Key Takeaways
  • Your brain predicts the future based on past experience, and it talks to you negatively about seven times more often than it does positively.
  • Avoidance goals ("stop doing X") feed that negative prediction. Approach goals ("start doing Y") work with your brain instead.
  • A goal you arrive at yourself gets far more commitment than one someone else handed you.
  • Progress is directional, not absolute. A hard day's small effort counts as real progress, not a setback.
  • Quitter's Day lands around week three because that's when unlearning an old habit collides with building a new one, and the mistakes that causes are necessary, not a sign to quit.

The next time a habit doesn't stick, resist the urge to file it under discipline. Look at how the goal was built instead. Was it something to avoid or something to chase? Was it handed to you, or did you actually choose it? Fix the design, and the same amount of willpower you already have starts working for you instead of against you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why do I keep failing at the same goal even when I try harder each time?

Trying harder usually means repeating the same goal design with more intensity, not fixing what's wrong with the design itself. If the goal is built as something to avoid, or as an outcome you can't feel successful about until it arrives, more effort just means more friction, not more success.

Is it normal to feel like I'm failing even when I'm doing everything right?

Yes. Progress happens in a nonlinear way, and a day that feels like a setback often isn't one, since you don't get to see the longer-term effect of what you're doing right now. Judging a single day in isolation is one of the most common ways people talk themselves out of progress that's actually happening.

Why does motivation disappear around the third week of a new habit?

Building a new habit as an adult requires unlearning an old pathway at the same time you're building a new one, and that process is where mistakes and friction show up. Most people interpret that friction as failure and quit right around week three, which is exactly what Quitter's Day tracks.

Does it matter if a goal was my idea versus someone else's suggestion?

It matters more than most people expect. A goal you arrive at yourself earns far more commitment than one handed to you by someone else, even a well-meaning spouse, doctor, or coach. If a goal isn't sticking, it's worth checking whether it's actually yours.
