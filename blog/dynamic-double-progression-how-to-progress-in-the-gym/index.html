The Simplest Way to Know When to Add Weight

Most people guess whether to increase weight or reps. Dynamic double progression removes the guesswork with one simple rule applied set by set.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· August 3, 2026 · 8 min read
Quick Answer

Increase weight only when you hit the top of your rep range on a given set, and treat every set independently instead of jumping the whole exercise up at once. This system is called dynamic double progression: you progress reps and weight as two separate variables, set by set, so you always know exactly when it's time to add load.

You finish a set of squats, rack the bar, and ask yourself the same question you've asked every week for months: do I go up in weight next time? You got 11 reps last week and 8 this week. Did you actually get stronger or weaker? Most people have no real system for answering that, so they guess, and guessing is why progress in the gym feels so random.

There's a simple, structured way to remove the guesswork entirely, and it's the exact system used with clients who need to know their training is actually working, not just hoping it is.

Why "3 Sets of 10" Is Quietly Sabotaging Your Progress

Most people walk into the gym with a fixed target: three sets of 10 reps, for example. The problem is that fixed rep targets hide how hard you're actually training. If you hit 10 reps on your first set, 10 on your second, and 10 on your third, you almost certainly left reps on the table on that first set. You could have pushed to 12 or 13 and simply stopped because a number in your head told you to.

A rep range instead of a fixed number, say 8 to 10 reps, fixes this immediately. It forces your reps to fluctuate naturally based on genuine effort, and it gives you a target to chase from week to week that isn't arbitrary.

The Concept Underneath All of This: Progressive Overload

Your body adapts to whatever stress you place on it. The exact weight and reps that felt hard three months ago now feel easy, and "easy" doesn't build muscle anymore. If you're using the same weight for the same reps you were using a year ago, you haven't built meaningful muscle in that year, no matter how consistently you've shown up.

Progressive overload just means the workouts have to get harder over time, whether through more weight, more reps, or more total volume. It's non-negotiable if building muscle is the goal. The part almost nobody has a system for is knowing exactly when and how to apply it.

Dynamic Double Progression: Reps and Weight as Two Separate Variables

Here's the system. "Double" means you're progressing two variables, reps and weight, rather than treating them as locked together. "Dynamic" means you treat every individual set independently instead of jumping the whole exercise up at once.

The rule is simple: you only increase weight on a set once you've hit the top of your rep range on that specific set. Every other set stays at its current weight until it earns the increase too.

1
Pick a rep range, not a fixed number
Choose something like 8 to 12 reps for an exercise instead of a flat "3x10." This gives you room to push each set to genuine effort.
2
Train each set to see where you land
Push close to failure on every set. Your reps will naturally decline set to set as fatigue builds, that's expected and correct.
3
Only increase weight on a set once it hits the top of the range
If your first set hits 12 reps but your second and third don't, only the first set's weight goes up next week. The others stay put until they hit 12 too.
4
Let the weight spread across sets, then converge
Over several weeks, each set independently earns its weight increase, until eventually all your sets catch up to the same heavier load.

Here's what that looks like across a few weeks on a barbell back squat, training in an 8 to 12 rep range.

Dynamic Double Progression in Practice: Back Squat
Week 1: 135 lbs11 / 10 / 8 reps
Week 2: 135 lbs (no set hit 12 yet)12 / 11 / 9 reps
Week 3: Set 1 up to 145 lbs, sets 2 & 3 stay at 1358 / 12 / 10 reps
Week 4: Sets 1 & 2 up to 145 lbs, set 3 stays at 135next progression
Weight and reps went up and down across different sets almost every week. That's not inconsistency, that's the system working exactly as designed.

Two Nuances That Make This Work Long Term

First, don't fixate on progressing only your first set. It's tempting because you're freshest there, but you won't PR your top set every single week forever. Sometimes the right move is pushing your second or third set an extra rep instead, since those sets carry fatigue from the ones before them and progress there counts just as much.

Second, train across a few different rep ranges in the same workout rather than using one range for everything. A heavy compound movement in the 4 to 6 rep range, a secondary movement in the 8 to 10 range, and an accessory movement in the 12 to 15 range keeps the stimulus varied and keeps training from getting stale, while applying this same progression model to every single one of them.

If you genuinely stall for 3 to 4 straight weeks with no progress on any set of a particular exercise, and you're not in a calorie deficit that would explain it, that's when it's worth changing the rep range on that lift or swapping the exercise entirely. Not before. Constantly switching exercises before giving this system time to work is what actually kills progress for most lifters.

Key Takeaways
  • Use a rep range like 8 to 12, not a fixed number like "3x10," so effort and fatigue can show up honestly in your numbers.
  • Only add weight to a set once that specific set hits the top of your rep range. Don't move the whole exercise up at once.
  • Treat every set independently. It's normal for different sets to be at different weights for weeks at a time.
  • If your first set stalls, look for progress on set two or three instead of forcing a PR that isn't there yet.
  • Only change rep ranges or swap exercises after 3 to 4 full weeks with zero progress on any set, not at the first sign of a plateau.

Progress in the gym stops feeling random the moment you stop asking "should I add weight?" as a guess and start answering it with a rule. Pick your rep range, train every set hard, and let each one earn its own weight increase. That's the entire system, and it's exactly why it works.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know when to increase weight in the gym?

Increase the weight on a specific set only once you've hit the top of your target rep range on that set. If you're training 8 to 12 reps and you hit 12 on your first set but not your second or third, only your first set's weight goes up next session.

Should all my sets use the same weight?

Not necessarily, especially while progressing. Dynamic double progression treats each set independently, so it's completely normal to have your first set at a heavier weight than your second and third for several weeks at a time.

What rep range should I train in to build muscle?

Most rep ranges between roughly 5 and 15 build muscle effectively when trained close to failure. Training a few different ranges across your exercises in the same workout, heavy, moderate, and light, adds variety while applying the same progression system to each.

How long should I wait before changing an exercise if I'm not progressing?

Give it 3 to 4 full weeks of true stalling, meaning zero progress on any set, before changing the rep range or swapping the exercise. Switching too frequently is one of the most common reasons lifters fail to make consistent progress.
