Is Fasted Cardio Bad for Women's Hormones?

You've heard fasted cardio will wreck your cortisol and hormones as a woman. Dr. Joey talks with Dr. Lauren Kenzo Simple, PhD in integrative physiology, about where that claim comes from and what the research actually shows.

Dr. Lauren Kenzo Simple, PhD
PhD in integrative physiology, co-owner of Mass Research Review, and an expert in women's health and exercise physiology.
Quick Answer

No, fasted cardio isn't inherently harmful to a healthy woman's hormones. It does raise cortisol slightly, but that's a normal, short-term response, not the chronic elevation linked to actual disease. The fear-based version of this claim overextends real medical findings onto people who don't have that condition.

You've almost certainly come across some version of this warning: as a woman, you should never train fasted, because it spikes cortisol and wrecks your hormones. It's stated with total confidence, usually with zero nuance, and it's enough to make anyone second-guess a workout they were otherwise fine doing.

I sat down with Dr. Lauren Kenzo Simple, who has her PhD in integrative physiology and specializes in women's health, to break down where this claim actually comes from and why it misrepresents what the research shows.

Where the Fear Around Cortisol Actually Comes From

The Claim

"You should absolutely never train fasted because it's going to increase your cortisol, wreak havoc on your hormones, increase body fat accumulation, and break down lean mass."

According to Dr. Kenzo Simple, chronically elevated cortisol is a real, documented problem, but specifically in the context of certain disease states that require medical attention. The issue is that this finding gets pulled out of that clinical context and applied as a blanket warning to otherwise healthy women who are simply exercising without eating first.

Cortisol fluctuates throughout the day as a completely normal part of your physiology. A temporary rise during a fasted workout isn't the same thing as the sustained, chronic elevation seen in the disease populations the original research was actually about.

Why This Kind of Claim Spreads So Easily

Dr. Kenzo Simple pointed to a pattern that shows up across women's fitness content generally: a small kernel of truth gets extrapolated into an absolute rule, stripped of the nuance and context that made the original finding meaningful in the first place.

The kernel of truth from that experience in a disease population is then being transitioned into messaging for otherwise healthy people. Cortisol fluctuations are normal.

This isn't unique to cortisol. The same pattern shows up in claims about nutrient timing, zone 2 cardio versus HIIT past a certain age, and a long list of "you must" or "you should never" rules that treat women's physiology as something requiring far more caution than the evidence actually supports.

The Real Cost of Over-Complicating Women's Fitness

According to Dr. Kenzo Simple, the biggest myth isn't any single claim, it's the broader theme that women's bodies are so different and so fragile that nearly everything requires a special rule. That framing does real damage, separate from whether any individual claim is technically true.

When fitness starts to feel like a minefield of things you must never do, with serious consequences implied for getting it wrong, the most common response isn't extra caution, it's avoidance. People disengage from fitness entirely rather than risk doing it the "wrong" way.

Key Takeaways
  • Fasted cardio does raise cortisol slightly, but that's a normal, temporary fluctuation, not chronic elevation.
  • The disease-state research behind the "cortisol wrecks your hormones" claim doesn't automatically apply to healthy women.
  • Much of the strictest women's fitness messaging extrapolates a real, narrow finding into an absolute, fear-based rule.
  • Real physiological differences exist, but they don't require the level of rigid caution often marketed online.
  • Overly rigid, fear-based rules tend to push people away from fitness entirely rather than making them safer.

The goal isn't ignoring real physiology, it's separating findings that apply to specific medical conditions from claims dressed up to sound universal. Most healthy women don't need a special exemption from fasted cardio, they need less fear-based noise clouding the actual decision.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is fasted cardio actually bad for women's hormones?

For most healthy women, no. Fasted cardio does raise cortisol slightly, but that's a normal, temporary fluctuation, not the kind of chronic elevation linked to disease. The claim that it wrecks hormones or destroys muscle overextends research from clinical populations onto otherwise healthy people.

Why does cortisol get such a bad reputation in women's fitness content?

Chronically elevated cortisol is genuinely linked to certain disease states that require medical attention. That real finding gets extrapolated into blanket warnings for healthy people, even though normal, temporary cortisol fluctuations from exercise or fasting aren't the same thing.

Do women need completely different fitness rules than men?

Not to the extreme degree often claimed online. While there are real physiological differences worth considering, much of the messaging that women must avoid fasted training, avoid certain cardio types, or follow rigid nutrient timing overcomplicates things well beyond what the evidence supports.

What's the risk of overly rigid, fear-based fitness rules for women?

When people feel like there's a strict right and wrong way to train and eat, with harsh consequences for getting it wrong, the more common outcome is that they avoid fitness altogether rather than risk doing it the wrong way.
