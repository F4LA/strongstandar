Is a High-Protein Diet Bad for Your Kidneys?

Your doctor sees your protein intake and warns you about your kidneys. Dr. Allan Bacon breaks down where that fear actually comes from, and why it usually doesn't apply to you.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· August 21, 2026 · 7 min read
AB
Dr. Allan Bacon
Nutrition scientist specializing in muscle physiology, health, and longevity.
Quick Answer

For someone with healthy kidneys, no, a high-protein diet is not dangerous. The fear around protein and kidney damage comes from research on people who already have kidney disease, and it gets misapplied to the general healthy population where it doesn't hold up the same way. This is general education, not medical advice, so talk to your doctor about your specific situation.

You increase your protein intake to support muscle growth, then your doctor sees it on your chart and warns you that too much protein is hard on your kidneys. It's one of the most common fears people run into once they start eating more protein than average, and according to Dr. Allan Bacon, a nutrition scientist specializing in muscle and longevity, it's largely based on a misapplication of the research.

The Claim

"That's too much protein, your kidneys are going to fail."

Where This Fear Actually Comes From

According to Dr. Bacon, the caution around high protein intake originates from nephrology, the medical specialty focused on kidney disease. In people who already have kidney damage or kidney failure, an excess of amino acids can genuinely be problematic, because damaged kidneys can't filter them efficiently.

That's true for someone with kidney damage. But it doesn't cause the damage itself, it exacerbates a problem when you're not filtering correctly in the first place.

The issue is that this guidance, meant specifically for an already-compromised population, often gets applied broadly to anyone eating a lot of protein, including people with completely normal kidney function, where the same risk doesn't apply.

Why Higher Protein Intakes Don't Show Health Hazards in Research

Dr. Bacon points to research looking at protein intakes well above standard recommendations, including intakes over 2 grams per pound of body weight sustained for over a year, that found no negative effects on kidney function in healthy people.

What can happen is a rise in certain blood markers, like BUN, blood urea nitrogen, which sometimes gets misread as a red flag by a doctor unfamiliar with how those markers behave at higher protein intakes.

Why a Higher BUN Reading Isn't Automatically a Problem

Protein contains nitrogen, and nitrogen gets excreted through urea. Eating more protein than the average person naturally means excreting more nitrogen, which shows up as a higher BUN reading on blood work. That's a predictable, non-pathological rise, not evidence that your kidneys are struggling.

Dr. Bacon draws a direct comparison to creatine supplementation, which reliably raises creatinine levels, another marker doctors sometimes misinterpret as a kidney concern, when it's simply a known, harmless byproduct of taking creatine.

What Reference Ranges Actually Represent

Standard biomarker ranges are built from general population data, and the general population doesn't typically eat anywhere near a gram of protein per pound of body weight. That means the "normal" range wasn't calibrated with someone eating that way in mind.

Dr. Bacon's recommendation for anyone facing pushback from a doctor about their protein intake: get a second opinion, ideally from a physician who works with athletes and is more familiar with how these markers behave in people who train seriously and eat accordingly.

Key Takeaways
  • The concern linking high protein to kidney damage comes from research on people who already have kidney disease.
  • Research on healthy individuals eating well above standard protein recommendations shows no negative effect on kidney function.
  • Higher BUN readings from a high-protein diet reflect increased nitrogen excretion, not kidney damage.
  • Standard reference ranges are based on general population data, which doesn't reflect people eating significantly more protein.
  • If a doctor raises concerns, a second opinion from a physician familiar with athletes can provide helpful context.

Understanding where this fear actually comes from doesn't mean ignoring your doctor. It means knowing enough about the research to ask informed questions and, if needed, seek a second opinion from someone who understands how these markers behave in people eating a higher-protein diet.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a high-protein diet bad for your kidneys?

For someone with healthy kidneys, no. The concern about protein and kidney damage comes from research on people who already have kidney disease, where excess amino acids can be harder to filter. That doesn't apply the same way to someone with normal kidney function.

Why do some doctors warn against high protein intake?

This often comes from applying nephrology guidance meant for existing kidney disease to the general healthy population, a misapplication of the research rather than an accurate reflection of what the evidence shows for healthy individuals.

Why does blood work show elevated BUN with a high-protein diet?

BUN reflects nitrogen excreted through urea, and protein contains nitrogen, so eating more protein than average naturally raises BUN. This is a non-pathological rise, not evidence of kidney damage, similar to how creatine intake raises creatinine without indicating harm.

What should you do if a doctor is concerned about your protein intake?

Get a second opinion, ideally from a doctor who works with athletes or is familiar with higher protein intakes, since general population reference ranges aren't necessarily calibrated for someone eating significantly more protein than average.
