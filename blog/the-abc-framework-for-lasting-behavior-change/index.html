The ABC Framework for Lasting Change

You've followed diets and workout plans before, but the motivation always fades eventually. Dr. Kasey Jo Orvidas breaks down the psychological framework behind motivation that actually sticks.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· September 3, 2026 · 8 min read

PhD in psychology specializing in behavior change, focused on how fixed and growth mindsets impact nutrition and exercise habits.
Quick Answer

Self-determination theory identifies three basic psychological needs that drive lasting motivation: autonomy (feeling in control of your decisions), belongingness (feeling part of a community), and competence (feeling capable and like you're improving). Supporting all three, referred to as the ABC framework, is a strong predictor of sticking with healthy habits long term.

Why do so many people fail to create lasting behavior change, even with a solid nutrition and training plan in hand? Dr. Kasey Jo Orvidas, a psychologist specializing in behavior change, points to a long-standing psychological theory that explains why motivation fades, and what actually sustains it.

The Three Needs Behind Lasting Motivation

Self-determination theory identifies three basic psychological needs required for someone to stay motivated to do something consistently, essentially forever.

NeedWhat It Means
AutonomyFeeling in control of your own decisions and part of the process, not just following orders
BelongingnessFeeling like you're part of a community, whether that's a gym, a coach relationship, or a group
CompetenceFeeling capable of doing the behavior and like you're learning or improving over time

Why "Just Tell Me What to Do" Doesn't Actually Work

Many people say they just want someone to tell them exactly what to do, believing that removes the burden of decision-making. But removing autonomy entirely tends to backfire, since feeling like a passive recipient of instructions rather than an active participant undermines the sense of control that sustains motivation.

People say all the time, I just need someone to tell me what to do, and it's like, but no, you don't. It just feels that way.

Why Feeling Like You Belong Changes Everything

People who walk into a gym and feel like they don't belong there, like they're not the "type" of person who fits in, are significantly less likely to keep coming back, even if they show up initially. Feeling genuinely part of a community, whether through a coach, a class, or training partners, substantially raises the odds of long-term consistency.

Why Feeling Competent Matters as Much as Results

Competence isn't just about achieving a specific result, it's about feeling capable of doing the behavior itself and sensing genuine progress along the way. Someone who feels perpetually incompetent at a new exercise or unable to understand nutrition basics is far more likely to give up, regardless of how good the underlying plan is.

Fixed vs. Growth Mindset: Why It Matters

A fixed mindset treats a missed workout or an off week as proof of failure, evidence you're simply "not the type" to succeed at this. A growth mindset treats the exact same setback as useful information, a signal to adjust and keep moving rather than a verdict on your capability.

This mindset shift connects directly back to the ABC framework. When you view struggles as normal and expected rather than as failures, competence stays intact, autonomy stays intact because you're still choosing to adjust rather than quit, and belonging stays intact because you're not isolating yourself out of shame.

Key Takeaways
  • Self-determination theory identifies autonomy, belongingness, and competence as the three needs behind lasting motivation.
  • Removing all autonomy, even when people ask for it, tends to undermine motivation rather than support it.
  • Feeling like you belong to a community significantly raises the odds of sticking with a new habit long term.
  • Competence is about feeling capable and improving over time, not just achieving a specific end result.
  • A growth mindset treats setbacks as useful information, protecting all three psychological needs at once.

If motivation has faded on you before, it's rarely a personal failing. It usually means one of these three needs, autonomy, belongingness, or competence, wasn't being genuinely supported by the plan or environment you were in.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the three psychological needs behind lasting motivation?

According to self-determination theory, the three basic psychological needs are autonomy (feeling in control of your own decisions), belongingness (feeling part of a community), and competence (feeling capable and like you're improving). All three need to be supported for motivation to last.

Why does being told exactly what to do often fail to keep people motivated?

Even though people often say they just want someone to tell them what to do, this removes the sense of autonomy that actually sustains motivation. Feeling like part of the decision-making process tends to support consistency better than simply following orders.

Why is feeling like you belong important for sticking with exercise?

People who don't feel like they belong in a gym or fitness community, even if they show up, are far less likely to keep coming back. A sense of belonging significantly increases the odds of maintaining a new habit long term.

What's the difference between a fixed and growth mindset in fitness?

A fixed mindset treats setbacks, like missing a workout or a protein target, as evidence of failure or being uncoachable. A growth mindset treats the same setback as useful information and an opportunity to adjust the approach and keep going.
