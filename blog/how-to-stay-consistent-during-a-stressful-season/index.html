How to Stay Consistent During a Stressful Season

You know life is about to get hard, and you're sure fitness is the first thing that will slip. Client Lowell Thompson shares the exact mindset shift that kept him consistent through one of the hardest seasons of his life.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· August 12, 2026 · 8 min read
LT
Lowell Thompson
High school associate principal, 27-year educator, and Fit for Life Academy client, sharing how he stayed consistent with his health while supporting his wife through cancer treatment.
Quick Answer

Staying consistent through a genuinely hard season comes down to lowering the bar to something sustainable, fitting movement and meals into the small windows you actually have, and shifting how you label yourself so a hard day doesn't feel like proof you've failed.

You already know a hard season is coming, or you're already in one, and you're bracing for fitness to be the first thing that falls apart. That's a reasonable fear. Most people's routines do fall apart under real stress, not because they lack discipline, but because the routine was never built to survive stress in the first place.

I sat down with Lowell Thompson, a client of ours and a 27-year educator, while his wife was going through chemotherapy, surgery, and radiation for breast cancer. What he shared about staying consistent through that season applies to any period where life gets genuinely hard, not just a health crisis.

Fit Your Habits Into the Windows You Actually Have

Lowell didn't have a fixed, protected training schedule during his wife's treatment. He trained when she was sleeping, planned meals when he had a few minutes, and let workouts get interrupted without treating the interruption as a failure.

The shift here isn't lowering your standards to nothing, it's accepting that a rigid, ideal schedule isn't available right now, and building around the smaller windows that are. A shortened workout that actually happens beats a full one that keeps getting postponed until it disappears entirely.

Taking Care of Yourself Isn't Selfish, It's What Makes You Able to Show Up

Lowell described feeling like he had to be constantly available for his wife, which is a familiar pull for anyone caring for someone they love. But he and his wife both recognized something important: he had to take care of himself in order to be able to take care of her.

I had to take care of myself in order to take care of her.

This isn't about balance in some abstract, aspirational sense. It's the practical reality that a depleted caregiver runs out of capacity faster. Protecting your own basic health during a hard season isn't a distraction from what matters, it's part of what makes showing up for someone else sustainable.

Change the Label Before You Try to Change the Behavior

The mindset shift Lowell described as the biggest turning point had nothing to do with meal plans. It was how he talked to himself after a slip. He used to call himself "an emotional eater," a label that turned every stressful moment into confirmation of who he already believed he was.

Instead, he started thinking of himself as a healthy eater who sometimes has an emotional eating moment. That's a small language shift with a large practical effect: a slip becomes an exception to your normal pattern instead of proof of your identity, which makes it far easier to just return to your normal habits the next day.

If I label myself an emotional eater, I'm an emotional eater. But if I label myself a healthy eater and I have some emotional eating, I can look at it from a more objective lens. It's not about perfection. It's about consistency.
Key Takeaways
  • Build your habits around the actual windows of time you have during a hard season, not an idealized schedule.
  • A shortened, interrupted workout that happens is more valuable than a perfect one that keeps getting postponed.
  • Taking care of your own health during caregiving isn't selfish, it's what sustains your ability to keep showing up.
  • How you label yourself after a slip matters. "A healthy eater who slipped" bounces back easier than "an emotional eater."
  • Consistency is about the overall pattern holding, not about achieving a flawless streak with zero exceptions.

The season that feels like it will end your consistency is often the exact season that teaches you what consistency actually requires: smaller windows, a lot of self-compassion, and a way of talking to yourself that doesn't turn a hard day into a verdict on who you are.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you stay consistent with fitness during a genuinely stressful season of life?

Shrink your commitments to what's actually sustainable given your capacity right now, use available windows of time instead of waiting for a perfect schedule, and shift how you label yourself, from someone managing a slip-up to someone who is generally consistent and occasionally sidesteps.

Is it selfish to prioritize your own health while caring for someone else?

No. Taking care of your own physical and mental health is what allows you to keep showing up for someone who depends on you. Sustained caregiving requires a caregiver who is also being taken care of.

What is the identity mindset shift for stopping emotional eating?

Instead of labeling yourself as "an emotional eater," which frames every slip as confirming your identity, you reframe yourself as a healthy eater who occasionally has an emotional eating moment. That reframe treats the behavior as an exception rather than a defining trait, which makes it easier to return to your normal pattern.

Does consistency mean never missing a workout or meal plan?

No. Consistency means the overall pattern holds even when individual days don't go as planned. It's about returning to your normal habits quickly after a disruption, not achieving a perfect streak with no exceptions.
