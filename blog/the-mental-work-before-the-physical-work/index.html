The Mental Work Before the Physical

You've followed diet plans before and they never stuck. Dad Bod Steve explains why the missing piece isn't a better workout, it's the mental work and planning that has to come first.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· September 4, 2026 · 7 min read
DS
Steve, "Dad Bod Steve"
Coach specializing in helping busy middle-aged men with demanding careers lose the "dad bod" without overhauling their entire lifestyle.
Quick Answer

Doing the mental work, clarifying why a goal actually matters and roughly planning what you'll eat each meal of the week, has to come before the physical work of training and dieting. Skipping this step is one of the biggest reasons people find short-term success with a diet but fail to sustain it long term.

Steve, known online as Dad Bod Steve, coaches busy middle-aged men trying to shed the classic "dad bod" without giving up their entire lifestyle. One of the biggest hurdles he sees isn't a bad workout program, it's skipping the mental work that has to happen before any plan can stick.

The Vision Exercise

With every new client, Steve runs a specific exercise before discussing any workout or diet plan. They talk about short-term goals, sure, losing 20 or 30 pounds, but then he pushes further: where do you want to be a decade from now? Thirty years from now? What happens if you continue on your current trajectory, and why does that matter to you specifically?

It's not like the secret, where if you put a Ferrari on a vision board it automatically ends up in your driveway. But when you remind yourself of your long-term goals regularly, you make it more of a priority.

Revisiting this vision regularly, not just once at the start, is what keeps a distant goal feeling like a genuine priority rather than a fading New Year's resolution.

Why Prioritization Alone Isn't Enough

Steve is clear that clarifying priorities doesn't automatically produce results. Even with a clear "why," if you don't plan accordingly, the priority doesn't translate into action. This is where the second piece of mental work comes in: mapping out your week.

The Weekly Meal-Mapping Ritual

Steve has clients look at their schedule and roughly plan every meal of the week, not necessarily full meal prep, just a rough idea of what they'll eat and when. Know you're going out to dinner Saturday, plan around it. Know you're eating at home the rest of the week, make sure the groceries are stocked so you're not defaulting to delivery on a busy Thursday night.

1
Look at your week ahead
Identify which nights involve eating out, social events, or anything outside your normal routine.
2
Roughly map every remaining meal
You don't need exact recipes, just a general idea of what you'll eat so mealtime doesn't arrive as a surprise.
3
Stock groceries around that plan
This prevents the common spiral of ordering delivery multiple nights simply because nothing was planned.
4
Spend just 5 to 10 minutes on this each Sunday
This small investment at the start of the week pays off across every single meal that follows.

Why "I Don't Know What to Eat" Is So Common

Steve points out that the classic client complaint, "I'm busy, so when lunchtime comes I don't know what to eat, so I just grab whatever's available," is a completely solvable problem, not a personal failing. It's simply the predictable result of never building the habit of even loosely planning meals in advance.

Key Takeaways
  • The vision exercise, looking 10 to 30 years ahead, helps a goal feel like a genuine priority rather than a passing intention.
  • Clarifying your "why" isn't enough on its own, it has to be followed by actual weekly planning to translate into results.
  • Roughly mapping out every meal for the week, without necessarily prepping, prevents last-minute food decisions driven by convenience alone.
  • Just 5 to 10 minutes of planning on a Sunday can prevent an entire week of reactive, off-plan eating.
  • Not knowing what to eat at mealtime is a planning problem, not a willpower problem, and it's fully solvable.

Before chasing a better workout split or a stricter diet, the mental work, clarifying why it matters and mapping out the week ahead, has to happen first. Skip that step, and even the best physical plan tends to fall apart the moment life gets busy.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the vision exercise for fitness goals?

It's an exercise where you look beyond a short-term goal like losing 20 pounds, and ask where you want to be in 10 or 30 years, and what will happen if you continue on your current trajectory. Revisiting this regularly helps a goal feel like a genuine priority rather than a passing intention.

What does "mental work before physical work" mean in fitness?

It means doing the prioritization and planning, deciding why a goal matters and roughly what you'll eat each day, before focusing on the physical execution of workouts or diet. Skipping this step is a major reason people struggle to follow through.

Do you need to meal prep every single meal in advance?

No. Simply mapping out roughly what you'll eat for each meal of the week, without necessarily prepping the food itself, removes the common problem of reaching lunchtime with no plan and grabbing whatever's available.

Why do so many people not know what they're going to eat at mealtime?

Most people never build the habit of planning meals even loosely in advance, so when a busy mealtime arrives without a plan, defaulting to whatever's convenient becomes the norm, regardless of whether it fits their actual goals.
