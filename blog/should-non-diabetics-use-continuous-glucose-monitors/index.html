Should Non-Diabetics Use a Glucose Monitor?

Continuous glucose monitors have gone from a medical device to a mainstream health trend. Dr. Joey breaks down what a blood sugar spike actually means, and why wearing one without diabetes can backfire.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· August 12, 2026 · 7 min read
Quick Answer

No, most non-diabetics don't need a continuous glucose monitor. Blood sugar spikes after eating carbohydrates are a normal, expected response, not a warning sign. Wearing a monitor to chase a flat line tends to encourage unhealthy eating patterns rather than genuinely improve your health.

Continuous glucose monitors used to be a medical tool exclusively for people managing diabetes. Now they're marketed as a general wellness device, something anyone curious about their metabolic health might strap on, regardless of whether they have any actual condition to monitor.

I'm Dr. Joey Munoz, and this question comes up often enough that I want to walk through it directly: if you're not diabetic, does watching your glucose levels in real time actually help you, or does it create a problem that wasn't there before?

A Blood Sugar Spike Is Supposed to Happen

When you eat carbohydrates, your blood sugar is supposed to rise, and your insulin is supposed to rise along with it to bring that sugar back down. This isn't a malfunction. It's your metabolism working exactly as designed.

A spike in blood sugar or insulin by itself isn't harmful. What actually signals a problem is when those levels stay chronically elevated over time, which is generally a downstream sign of something like chronic inflammation affecting insulin sensitivity, not a normal post-meal spike that resolves on its own.

Why Watching the Number in Real Time Changes Your Behavior

The Concern

"If you're constantly monitoring your glucose and trying to avoid anything that spikes it, you'll avoid triggers by any means, and some of those means aren't necessarily healthy."

This is the practical problem with strapping a real-time monitor onto a normal, healthy body. Once you can see the number moving, the instinct is to control it, and the fastest way to flatten a glucose curve is to eliminate the foods most likely to raise it, regardless of whether those foods are actually good for you.

That's exactly how a monitor can nudge someone toward a very low-carbohydrate or ketogenic style of eating, not because it's the healthiest overall pattern, but because it produces the flattest, most reassuring-looking graph.

Whole-Food Carbs Raise Blood Sugar and Still Support Good Health

This is the part that gets lost when the goal becomes "avoid the spike" instead of "eat well." Diets higher in whole-food carbohydrates, fruits, vegetables, whole grains, are consistently linked to strong health outcomes in the research, even though eating them absolutely does raise blood sugar and insulin.

A monitor doesn't distinguish between a blood sugar rise from an apple and a rise from candy. It just shows a number going up. Without the fuller context of what a food actually offers nutritionally, that number alone becomes a poor guide for what to eat.

Key Takeaways
  • Blood sugar and insulin are supposed to rise after eating carbohydrates. That response is normal, not harmful.
  • Chronic elevation, not a normal post-meal spike, is the actual sign of an underlying issue worth addressing.
  • Watching your glucose in real time tends to push people toward eliminating foods just to flatten the graph.
  • Whole-food carbohydrates like fruit and whole grains raise blood sugar and are still associated with strong health outcomes.
  • For most non-diabetics, a continuous glucose monitor creates a problem to manage rather than solving one that existed.

If you don't have diabetes, your energy is generally better spent building a diet around whole foods, adequate protein, and enough fiber, not watching a real-time number that was never designed to guide a healthy person's eating in the first place.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should you use a continuous glucose monitor if you're not diabetic?

Generally, no. For most non-diabetics, a continuous glucose monitor tends to encourage avoiding normal blood sugar fluctuations, which can push people toward less healthy eating patterns, like avoiding fruit and whole grains, in order to keep the number flat.

Is a blood sugar spike after eating actually bad for you?

No, a blood sugar spike after eating carbohydrates is a completely normal physiological response, not a sign of harm. It only becomes a concern when glucose and insulin are chronically elevated, which usually points to an underlying issue like chronic inflammation, not the spike itself.

Why can chasing flat blood sugar readings backfire?

Trying to avoid any blood sugar spike can push someone toward very low-carbohydrate approaches, like strict keto, that minimize glucose response but may not be the healthiest overall pattern, since high-fiber, whole-food carbohydrate sources are associated with strong health outcomes despite raising blood sugar.

Are fruits and whole grains bad because they spike blood sugar?

No. Diets higher in whole-food carbohydrates like fruits, vegetables, and whole grains are consistently associated with positive health outcomes, even though they do raise blood sugar and insulin after eating. That fluctuation is expected and not something to eliminate.
