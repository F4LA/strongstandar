Regressive Overload: Hard Seasons

You know progressive overload, but what about the opposite? Jordan Syatt shares how doing intentionally less, not more, kept him consistent through the hardest months of new parenthood.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· September 4, 2026 · 7 min read
JS
Jordan Syatt
Online fitness coach with a background in powerlifting, and father who has navigated fitness through demanding stretches of early parenthood.
Quick Answer

Regressive overload means intentionally doing less than your usual training, not to build toward a personal record, but simply to keep the habit alive during a genuinely hard season of life. Jordan Syatt kept doing 10 to 20-minute workouts at midnight or 1am for months, prioritizing consistency over quality until his circumstances improved.

You know progressive overload, gradually doing more to keep improving. Jordan Syatt introduces the opposite concept for hard seasons of life: regressive overload, doing intentionally less, not to progress, but simply to survive and keep the habit alive.

What Regressive Overload Looked Like for Him

During the first few brutal months of early parenthood, Jordan kept training, but not in any way resembling his usual sessions. He'd work out at midnight or 1am for months, and by his own admission, the workouts weren't good. They weren't personal record attempts, they weren't progressively overloaded, in fact they were the opposite: regressively overloaded, shorter and easier than what he'd normally do.

It's not as much of a personal record workout. There's not much progressive overload, in fact there's a lot of regressive overload. It's just focused on keeping the ball rolling.

Sometimes that meant a 10-minute workout. Sometimes 20 minutes. The specific length or intensity wasn't the point, doing something nearly every day was, because he'd found that skipping it entirely left him feeling noticeably worse, both physically and as a husband, father, and business owner.

Why Different People Prioritize Different Things Under Stress

Interestingly, Jordan and Dr. Joey handled the tradeoffs differently during their own hard seasons. For Jordan, movement was the non-negotiable priority, while his nutrition suffered noticeably, more processed food, less of the "good stuff" like fruit. For Dr. Joey, with less time for structured workouts during a demanding stretch with young kids, nutrition became the area he controlled most tightly instead.

Jordan's Priority

Movement above all else. Nutrition was allowed to slip during the hardest stretches, since exhaustion made cooking and planning harder.

Dr. Joey's Priority

Nutrition as the controllable variable when workout time was scarce, since meals could still be planned even without gym time.

Neither approach is inherently right or wrong. What matters is being honest about which area you can realistically sustain when life gets genuinely difficult, rather than trying to white-knuckle every variable at once and burning out on all of them.

Why This Mindset Matters Beyond Parenting

Any major life event, a demanding work project, an illness, a family crisis, can create the same kind of constraint. The instinct to wait for a "clean" period before resuming healthy habits often means waiting indefinitely, since life rarely offers a perfectly uncomplicated stretch. Regressive overload offers a middle path: keep the habit alive in a smaller form, rather than abandoning it entirely until conditions improve.

Key Takeaways
  • Regressive overload means intentionally doing less during a hard season, purely to keep a habit alive, not to progress.
  • A short, imperfect workout is more valuable during difficult stretches than an all-or-nothing approach that leads to skipping entirely.
  • Different people naturally default to protecting different areas, training or nutrition, under stress, and either can work.
  • Waiting for a "clean" period to resume healthy habits often means waiting indefinitely, since life rarely offers one.
  • The goal during a hard season is survival and momentum, not personal records or ideal execution.

Progressive overload gets all the attention in fitness content, but regressive overload might be the more important skill for actually staying consistent through the parts of life that don't cooperate with a perfect plan.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is regressive overload?

Regressive overload is intentionally doing less than your usual training volume or intensity during a genuinely hard season of life, not to build toward a personal record, but simply to maintain the habit and momentum until circumstances improve.

Is it okay to do a 10-minute workout instead of skipping entirely?

Yes. During genuinely difficult stretches, a short, imperfect workout that keeps the habit alive is more valuable than an all-or-nothing mindset that leads to skipping entirely until life calms down, which for many people never fully happens.

Should you prioritize training or nutrition when you can't do both well?

There's no single right answer. Different people naturally default to protecting different areas under stress, some prioritize movement first, others prioritize nutrition first, and either approach can work as long as you're honest about which one is realistically sustainable for you.

Why does having kids change how someone approaches fitness?

Time and energy become genuinely limited in a way they weren't before, especially in the early months. This makes minimum-effective-dose thinking, keeping the habit alive with less rather than abandoning it, a more realistic survival strategy than trying to maintain a pre-kids routine unchanged.
