5 Training Mistakes Limiting Your Growth

You're lifting consistently, but your muscle growth has stalled. Dr. Joey breaks down 5 common mistakes that quietly limit results, even when you're showing up and putting in the work.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· August 29, 2026 · 8 min read
Quick Answer

Five common mistakes quietly limit muscle growth: doing identical reps across every set instead of pushing intensity, resting too little between sets, avoiding machines entirely, not training through a full range of motion, and switching up exercises too frequently to ever build proficiency.

Simply lifting weights doesn't mean you're optimally building muscle. Most people, even ones training consistently, are making at least one of these five mistakes without realizing it. Here's what to fix.

Mistake One: Doing the Same Reps for Every Set

If you do three sets of 10 reps using the same weight for all three, you're likely leaving reps in the tank on that first set. When you train with sufficient intensity, your reps should naturally decrease slightly from set to set, maybe 10, then 9, then 8, as fatigue accumulates. If you're hitting the exact same rep count across all sets, your earlier sets weren't pushed hard enough.

Mistake Two: Resting Too Little Between Sets

Resting only one or two minutes between sets limits both your performance and your muscle growth. For heavy compound movements like squats, deadlifts, and presses, rest 3 to 5 minutes. You should feel genuinely gassed after a hard set, if you feel ready to go again after a minute, you likely aren't training with enough intensity.

If after resting for a minute you feel like you're ready to go for another set, take a look at your intensity, because you're not pushing hard enough.

For isolation movements like curls, tricep extensions, or lateral raises, 2 to 3 minutes is typically sufficient, since these movements are less taxing on your central nervous system.

Mistake Three: Only Using Free Weights

Big free weight compound movements are taxing not just to your muscles but to your entire nervous system. Doing several difficult free weight exercises back to back means you're fatigued by the later ones, hurting your performance on movements that could otherwise be productive.

Using a machine, like a chest-supported row, for secondary movements offers more stability, which makes it easier to squeeze out effort on an already-fatigued muscle without needing to also manage balance and stabilization.

Mistake Four: Not Training Through a Full Range of Motion

Training with a full range of motion, and maximally stretching the target muscle at the bottom of each rep, is one of the most important variables for muscle growth outside of intensity itself. A squat should go to depth. A pull-up should reach a full dead hang. A dumbbell press should bring the weights past your chest.

Full range of motion increases muscle fiber recruitment, mechanical tension, and metabolic stress, all contributors to muscle growth. If you can't complete an exercise through its full range with good control, the weight is likely too heavy.

Mistake Five: Alternating Exercises Too Frequently

Switching your entire exercise selection every few weeks makes it nearly impossible to track progressive overload, since you can't fairly compare performance across different movements. It also prevents skill mastery: performing an exercise like a squat correctly is a skill that takes time to develop, and constantly changing exercises means you never fully develop the efficiency needed to maximally fatigue the target muscle.

Key Takeaways
  • Reps should naturally decrease slightly across sets if you're training with adequate intensity.
  • Rest 3-5 minutes for heavy compound lifts and 2-3 minutes for isolation movements to protect performance.
  • Combining free weights with machines, especially for later exercises, protects quality when fatigue sets in.
  • Training through a full range of motion, with a maximal stretch, drives more muscle growth than partial reps.
  • Sticking with core exercises for 6-8 weeks lets you measure real progress and build technical proficiency.

None of these fixes require an overhaul of your training program. Adjusting rest periods, range of motion, and exercise consistency can meaningfully improve results from the same overall effort you're already putting in.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should you rest between sets to build muscle?

For heavy compound movements like squats, deadlifts, and presses, rest 3 to 5 minutes between sets. For isolation movements like curls or lateral raises, 2 to 3 minutes is typically enough. Resting too little limits both your performance and your muscle growth.

Should every set use the same number of reps?

No. If you train with sufficient intensity, your reps should naturally decrease slightly from set to set as fatigue builds. Doing the exact same number of reps across all sets with the same weight usually means the earlier sets weren't pushed hard enough.

Does training through a full range of motion actually matter for muscle growth?

Yes. Training through a full range of motion, especially maximally stretching the muscle at the bottom of a movement, increases muscle fiber recruitment, mechanical tension, and metabolic stress, all of which contribute to more muscle growth than partial range of motion.

How often should you change your exercises?

Changing exercises too frequently makes it hard to track progressive overload and prevents you from developing the skill and neuromuscular efficiency needed to fully fatigue a muscle. Sticking with core exercises for at least 6 to 8 weeks allows for measurable progress and better technique.
