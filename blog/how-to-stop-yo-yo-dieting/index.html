How to Stop Yo-Yo Dieting for Good

Losing and regaining the same weight isn't a willpower problem. It's a strategy problem. Here's what actually breaks the cycle, for good.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· June 22, 2026 · 8 min read

You've been here before. Something finally clicks, you get motivated, you cut calories, you start running, the weight starts coming off, and for a few weeks or even a few months, it feels like this time is different. Then life gets busy, you eat something you "shouldn't," and slowly the weight comes back. A year later, you're starting over again.

If that cycle describes the last 5, 10, or 20 years of your life, I want to be clear about something: this is not a willpower problem. It is a strategy problem. And strategy can be fixed.

In this episode, I sit down with Nate Waldron, a client who has been part of our community for over two years. Nate grew up obese, spent decades yo-yo dieting, never felt comfortable taking his shirt off in public, and had tried more programs and coaches than he can count. He's now down over 45 pounds and, for the first time in his life, is building a body and an identity he's proud of.

The Cycle That Keeps Repeating

Yo-yo dieting has a very predictable structure. Once you see it clearly, you understand exactly why every attempt that uses the same approach produces the same result.

1
The trigger
Discomfort reaches a tipping point. A photo, a health scare, a comment, a number on the scale. Something makes you decide today is the day you change.
2
The restriction
You go all-in. Dramatic calorie cuts, hours of cardio, eliminating entire food groups. Motivation is high and the scale moves fast.
3
The relief
You reach a weight that feels acceptable. The urgency fades. The strict approach slowly loosens because the discomfort that started it is gone.
4
The drift
Without the habits that produced the results, the results disappear. You're back at the beginning, often heavier than where you started.

Nate lived this cycle for decades. He lost weight living in Spain because he was walking everywhere. He came home and gained it all back. He ran half marathons and was still, in his words, "out-eating his running." The tool was working. The foundation underneath it wasn't there.

The problem with every quick-fix approach is that when you stop doing it, the results stop with it. You haven't built anything that lasts. You've just borrowed results from a temporary behavior.

Why Cardio Alone Will Not Get You There

One of the most common patterns I see is people using exercise as punishment for eating. You ate too much, so now you run it off. This creates a deeply dysfunctional relationship with both food and training, and it almost never produces lasting change.

Nate ran over 55 races across 15 years and still struggled with his weight throughout. He loved running. Running wasn't the problem. The problem was using cardio as the primary lever for body composition without ever addressing what was actually driving his eating.

Exercise should never be a punishment. It is a tool to build a better version of yourself, completely independent of how well you ate that day.

When he started building muscle alongside his running, two things happened. First, his body composition changed in a way that cardio alone had never produced. Second, and more importantly for a runner, his performance improved dramatically. He hit a lifetime personal record in the half marathon, cutting over 13 minutes off his time. Not because he ran more, but because he was stronger and carrying less weight.

More muscle also means more metabolic flexibility. When you have a higher lean mass, your body burns more calories at rest, your daily calorie budget is larger, and occasional indulgences don't move the needle the way they once did. One bowl of ice cream with your wife on a hard day isn't a problem when you've built the foundation to absorb it.

The Real Driver: Your Relationship With Food

Nate grew up with what he describes as a scarcity mindset around food. Born premature, he believes his mother equated feeding him with care and love. That wiring stayed with him. As an adult, he would hide ice cream in the back of the freezer. If his brother found it and ate some, Nate would pour the rest down the sink rather than share it. The logic was: if I can't have all of it, no one gets any.

That pattern, the feeling that food might disappear so you need to take it now, is extraordinarily common. It shows up as eating past fullness at every meal, snacking out of habit rather than hunger, finishing everything on the plate regardless of how you feel. Most people who struggle with their weight are not lazy or undisciplined. They are responding to a set of deeply ingrained behaviors that were built over years, often decades, often starting in childhood.

You don't wake up one day and those patterns are gone. You build better tools to manage them. That's the actual work.

Nate is still aware of that voice in his head telling him to take the cinnamon roll before it's gone. What's changed is that he now has the awareness to recognize it for what it is, and the habits to navigate it without letting it derail everything else.

Tracking Is Awareness, Not Obsession

One of the biggest things that shifted Nate's trajectory was calorie tracking. Not as a permanent sentence, but as a tool to build awareness he had never had before.

For most of his adult life, Nate ate more than he needed at every meal. Not by a massive amount, but consistently, across years. He didn't know that because he had never measured it. When you don't have a clear picture of what you're actually doing, you can't make an accurate adjustment. You end up going from zero structure to extreme restriction and back, because there's no middle ground you can see.

Tracking creates that middle ground. It shows you that a serving of cereal is not the full bowl. It shows you that the snacking you're doing at night adds up to more than an entire meal. It makes the invisible visible, which is the only way you can change it accurately.

The research on long-term weight maintenance is clear: people who successfully keep weight off track something consistently. It doesn't have to be calories forever. For me personally, I haven't tracked calories in over a decade. But I do track my weight, my protein intake, my steps, and my training. That's enough to maintain awareness and course-correct early when things drift.

Nate eventually built a custom AI agent to do the tracking conversationally because the friction of apps was getting in his way. The point isn't the method. The point is maintaining some form of awareness that keeps you anchored to reality. Find what works for you and use it consistently.

Outcomes Follow Process, Not the Other Way Around

The thing that makes Nate's story different from his previous attempts isn't that he found a harder program or was more disciplined. It's that he stopped chasing the outcome and started focusing on the process.

When he started, his goal was to weigh 195 lbs. He hit it. He still didn't look the way he wanted. His new goal became 185. Then 175. The number on the scale kept moving because the habits underneath it kept building. The physique was a byproduct of the process, not the target.

What Nate chose instead of a 90-day transformation promise was a program with no end date and no guaranteed result in a specific timeframe. His wife told him she didn't think he'd stick with it. What changed was that this time, the focus was on building the habits, not hitting the number. And habits don't disappear when a program ends because they've become part of who you are.

We overestimate what we can do in three months and we dramatically underestimate what we can do in two to three years.

Two years is not a long time. In two years, Nate went from running in the dark because he didn't want anyone to see him to taking his shirt off in Costa Rica in front of his kids. From hiding his workouts behind a locked door to training with his son in the same room. From a lifetime of yo-yo dieting to a foundation he's confident he'll never abandon.

Why That Matters Beyond the Scale

One of the questions I ask every client is how their fitness has impacted the other areas of their life. The answers are almost never about the scale.

For Nate, getting his body right changed how he shows up as a husband. He had been emotionally closed off partly because he was uncomfortable with himself. He wouldn't take his shirt off in front of his wife. He avoided situations that made him feel exposed. When that changed, his presence in the relationship changed with it.

It changed how he shows up as a father. His 12-year-old son rides his bike alongside him on runs. His 15-year-old daughter asks him for training advice. His youngest asks to join in on upper body days. These are moments that exist because Nate decided to become someone different, and his kids are watching it happen.

And it changed how he shows up as a coach. Nate helps people navigate difficult challenges professionally. He told me he could coach others through anything but couldn't coach himself. That disconnect is gone. He is now the same man in every room, which is the personal vision statement he reads to himself every morning.

Key Takeaways
  • Yo-yo dieting is a strategy problem, not a willpower problem. Restricting without building habits produces results that disappear when the restriction ends.
  • Cardio alone will not produce lasting body composition change. Building muscle increases your metabolic baseline and gives you more flexibility with your diet long-term.
  • The emotional relationship with food is real and worth addressing. Awareness of your patterns is the first step to managing them without eliminating all enjoyment.
  • Tracking is a tool for building awareness, not a life sentence. Use it long enough to understand your actual behaviors, then keep tracking whatever variables matter most for your maintenance.
  • Physique change is a byproduct of consistent habits. When the habits are in place, the results follow and stay, because you've built the identity that produces them.

The path off the yo-yo isn't a better diet or a harder program. It's building the habits, the awareness, and the identity of someone who takes care of their body as a non-negotiable. That takes longer than 90 days. It also lasts longer than any program you've ever tried.
