How Strict Should Your Diet Actually Be?

Andrew Huberman says just eat meat, fish, eggs, fruit, and vegetables, nothing else. Dr. Joey breaks down why that works at first, why it usually backfires, and the real skill you need to keep weight off for good.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· August 6, 2026 · 9 min read
Quick Answer

Very strict diets, like eating only meat, fish, eggs, fruit, and vegetables, do work at first because they create an automatic calorie deficit. The problem is long term: restriction creates friction with normal life, and 80 to 90 percent of weight regain is linked to overly restrictive approaches. The real goal isn't finding the strictest diet. It's building the skill of maintaining a flexible one.

You've probably heard some version of this: cut out everything except meat, fish, eggs, fruit, and vegetables, and the weight falls off. Andrew Huberman recently gave that exact advice, and if you've tried it, you know it's true. You lose weight fast. But you've probably also lived the other half of that story: a few months later, it's all back, plus a little more.

I'm Dr. Joey Munoz, nutrition science PhD, and I want to walk through exactly why ultra-strict diets work so well at first, why they tend to fail long term, and what you actually need to learn instead if you want to lose weight and keep it off for good.

Why Strict, Whole-Food Diets Work, At First

When you only eat whole foods like meat, fish, eggs, fruit, and vegetables, you fall into what's called an autonomic calorie deficit. You're eating fewer calories without ever tracking a single one. This isn't magic. It happens because whole foods have a higher satiety score than processed foods, meaning they fill you up more per calorie. A boiled potato and a bag of potato chips can have similar calories, but one is genuinely hard to overeat and the other is dangerously easy.

This is actually a strategy I use with new clients. During the first 8 to 12 weeks of coaching, we don't track a single calorie. We just build behaviors around protein, fiber, and whole foods, and clients frequently lose 10 to 15 pounds without ever counting anything.

Why the Same Approach Backfires Long Term

Here's the part that gets left out of the "just eat clean" advice. Restriction causes friction, and friction eventually causes failure. The more foods you label as off limits, the harder it becomes to stay consistent the moment life doesn't cooperate: a birthday with cake, a weekend with friends, a vacation with a buffet.

The research backs this up clearly. 80 to 90 percent of weight regain is linked to a lack of structure and overly restrictive methods, not a lack of knowledge about how to lose weight in the first place. Most people who regain weight already know how to lose it. They've done it multiple times. What they've never learned is how to maintain it.

You don't have a weight loss problem. You have a weight maintenance skills problem.

The Skill Nobody Talks About: Maintenance

Losing weight and keeping it off are two different skill sets. Anybody can white-knuckle a strict diet for 6 to 8 weeks and lose 15 to 20 pounds. The real win is staying in shape while living a normal life, going to family parties, eating a slice of pizza without turning it into the whole pizza, and getting back to normal the next meal instead of writing off the entire day.

Restrictive Approach
  • Certain foods are labeled "off limits" completely
  • One slip feels like total failure
  • Requires perfect discipline every single day
  • Collapses around birthdays, vacations, and holidays
Flexible Approach
  • All foods can fit, just not unlimited amounts
  • An off day is corrected at the next meal, not "Monday"
  • Tighten up or loosen up depending on the week
  • Built to survive real life, not just a lab setting

Learning to Tighten Up Without Going to Extremes

Part of this skill is developing an intuitive sense of portions instead of weighing every gram forever. I personally fluctuate between roughly 212 and 217 pounds. When I creep toward the higher end, I don't go into diet mode. I just tighten things up slightly: a bit more consistent with meals, a bit more active, until I settle back down. Then I loosen up again. That's a completely different mindset than "I'm either perfect with my diet or not at all."

This same flexibility matters just as much around why your diet falls apart on weekends or during the holidays. The people who struggle most for years are almost always the ones trying to be the most restrictive, not the ones being the most flexible.

Key Takeaways
  • Strict whole-food diets work at first because they create an autonomic calorie deficit without any calorie tracking.
  • Restriction creates friction, and 80 to 90 percent of weight regain is linked to overly restrictive, unstructured approaches.
  • Losing weight and maintaining weight loss are two different skills. Most people already know how to lose weight.
  • Maintenance requires portion awareness, emotional regulation around food, and recovering quickly after an off day.
  • A diet that collapses the moment you eat a slice of bread isn't a diet built to last.

Strict diets aren't the enemy. They can be a useful starting point for a few weeks. But if the goal is a body and a lifestyle that lasts, the real work isn't finding a stricter set of rules. It's learning how to stay consistent inside a normal life, one that includes birthdays, dinners out, and the occasional slice of cake.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a strict diet like eating only meat, fish, eggs, fruit, and vegetables actually work for weight loss?

Yes, and it works fast. Whole foods are more filling per calorie than processed foods, so cutting your diet down to a short list of whole foods creates what's called an autonomic calorie deficit: you eat less without ever tracking a single calorie.

Why do restrictive diets often lead to regaining the weight?

Restriction creates friction. When every birthday, date night, or vacation puts you around foods you've labeled off limits, staying consistent becomes exhausting. Research shows 80 to 90 percent of weight regain is linked to overly restrictive, unstructured approaches rather than a lack of knowledge about how to lose weight.

What's the difference between losing weight and keeping it off?

Losing weight is a discipline problem you can white-knuckle through for 6 to 8 weeks. Keeping it off is a completely different skill set: portion awareness, emotional regulation around food, and the ability to recover quickly from a single off day instead of spiraling into a full relapse.

How do I make my diet more flexible without losing progress?

Build most of your diet around whole foods, but stop treating any single food as forbidden. Learn to have a slice of cake at a party and simply return to your normal eating the next meal, rather than treating one indulgence as a reason to abandon the day.
