Power Matters More Than Strength With Age

You've focused on getting stronger, but strength alone may not be what keeps you steady on your feet. Dr. Brent Momb, a muscle biologist, explains why contraction speed might matter more than raw force as you age.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· August 29, 2026 · 7 min read
BM
Dr. Brent Momb
PhD in muscle biology, postdoctoral fellow at the University of Massachusetts studying the molecular mechanisms behind muscle function and aging.
Quick Answer

Muscle power, which depends heavily on how fast a muscle contracts, appears to matter more than raw strength for preventing falls as you age. Research shows contractile velocity slows with age even in cases where raw force stays the same or increases, making the ability to react quickly a distinct variable worth training for directly.

Most conversations about aging and muscle focus on strength: how much weight you can lift, how much force you can produce. Dr. Brent Momb, a muscle biologist studying the molecular mechanisms of aging muscle, points to a different variable that may matter just as much, or more, for staying steady on your feet: how fast your muscles can actually contract.

Why Protein Turnover Slows With Age

Muscle function depends on a constant cycle of protein synthesis and breakdown. When that balance shifts toward more breakdown than synthesis, muscle fibers shrink and overall muscle size decreases. But there's a second, less discussed effect: as protein turnover slows with age, proteins involved in muscle contraction stick around longer without being renewed, allowing chemical modifications to accumulate that change how those proteins function.

Specifically, this affects how quickly myosin and actin, the two proteins responsible for muscle contraction, bind together and release. When that interaction slows down at the molecular level, it scales up to the entire muscle, slowing overall contraction speed.

The Surprising Finding in Older Women

Here's where it gets counterintuitive: in some populations, particularly older females, single-fiber force production doesn't decline with age, it can actually increase. That happens because when myosin and actin stay bound together longer, they become stiffer and produce more force.

What Increases

Single-fiber force production can rise in older females, since proteins staying bound longer produces more stiffness and force.

What Declines

Contractile velocity, how fast the muscle can actually contract, slows down significantly, reducing power output.

We might see maximum contractile velocities are slowed, especially in older females. That's a big determining factor as to why strength is not the only correlate to whole muscle function.

Why This Matters More Than Raw Strength for Falls

Power, not just force, depends heavily on contractile velocity, and power is what determines your ability to react quickly, like catching yourself with your leg when you trip, or grabbing something to stop a fall. A muscle that's strong but contracts slowly may not be able to produce force fast enough in that split-second moment.

This distinction matters for how training should be approached with age. Strength training alone, focused on lifting heavier weight slowly and with control, may not sufficiently target the contraction speed that actually protects against falls.

Key Takeaways
  • Muscle power, driven largely by contraction speed, appears more directly linked to fall prevention than raw strength.
  • Slower protein turnover with age lets contractile proteins undergo changes that reduce how fast they bind and release.
  • In some populations, like older women, force production can increase even as contraction speed and overall power decline.
  • The ability to react quickly during a stumble depends more on contractile velocity than maximum force output.
  • Training that emphasizes moving weight with speed, not just lifting heavy slowly, more directly targets power and contraction velocity.

Getting stronger as you age still matters, but it isn't the whole picture. Training your muscles to contract quickly, not just to produce maximum force, may be the missing piece for staying steady and independent for decades to come.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is muscle strength or muscle power more important for preventing falls?

Does muscle strength always decline with age?

Not always in a straightforward way. In some populations, particularly older females, single-fiber force production can actually increase with age, even as overall muscle function and contraction speed decline.

Why does muscle contraction speed slow down with age?

Slower protein turnover in aging muscle allows proteins involved in contraction to undergo changes that alter their function, causing the myosin and actin proteins responsible for muscle contraction to bind and release more slowly.

What kind of training targets muscle power specifically?

Training that emphasizes moving weight quickly, not just lifting heavy weight slowly, targets contractile velocity and power more directly than traditional slow, controlled strength training alone.
