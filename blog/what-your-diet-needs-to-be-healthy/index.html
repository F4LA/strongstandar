What Makes a Diet Actually Healthy?

You've tried the diets that tell you exactly what to eat and what to avoid, and none of them stuck. Dr. Joey breaks down the 5 things every healthy diet actually needs to do, and why most popular diets are missing at least one.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· August 10, 2026 · 8 min read
Quick Answer

A truly healthy diet has to do five things: meet your nutritional needs, help regulate your hunger, be enjoyable, support your specific goals, and be sustainable for your actual lifestyle. Most popular diets fail because they're missing at least one of these, not because you lack discipline.

Ask ten different experts what a healthy diet looks like and you'll get ten different answers. Keto, carnivore, vegan, low-fat, high-protein, intermittent fasting. Each one comes with a confident set of rules about what to eat and what to avoid, and each one claims to be the answer.

I'm Dr. Joey Munoz, and I want to shift how you think about this entirely. A healthy diet isn't a list of good foods and bad foods. It's a set of five principles, and if your current approach is missing even one of them, that's usually the real reason it hasn't stuck.

It Has to Meet Your Nutritional Needs

This means both macronutrients, protein, carbs, and fats, and micronutrients, your vitamins and minerals. Here's where most restrictive diets fail immediately: eliminating entire food groups, like all animal products or all grains, cuts you off from nutrients that are concentrated in those specific foods and hard to get elsewhere.

The same applies to going too low in calories overall. Dropping from 2,000 calories to 1,200 doesn't just create a deficit, it makes it far harder to hit your protein target, your fiber, and your micronutrient needs at the same time. The fix isn't eliminating food groups for no medical reason. It's eating a wider variety of whole foods, unless you have an actual allergy or sensitivity.

It Has to Help You Manage Hunger

A diet that leaves you starving isn't a diet you'll stick to, no matter how disciplined you think you are. Hunger and satiety come down to three levers: how much you're eating overall, which foods you're choosing, and when you're eating.

On overall intake, a slight to moderate deficit of roughly 10 to 20% below maintenance is far more sustainable than an aggressive cut. On food choices, whole foods keep you fuller than processed ones even when the calories and macros are identical, largely because of differences in mouthfeel and how easy they are to overeat. On timing, eating more earlier in the day and less late at night tends to support better hunger regulation.

It Has to Be Enjoyable

You will not stick to a diet you hate, and you shouldn't try to. The idea that health requires constant willpower and suffering is backwards. The foods that satisfy your nutritional needs can look completely different from person to person, shaped by culture, upbringing, and personal preference.

You don't need to be more disciplined so you can stick to a diet you hate. You don't need to follow that diet at all.

This doesn't mean eating whatever you want in any amount. It means meeting the same nutritional targets, whole foods, adequate protein, enough fiber, with the specific foods you actually enjoy eating.

It Has to Support Your Specific Goals

There's no single perfect diet, because your goal changes what "optimal" looks like. Someone building muscle needs more protein and enough total calories to support growth. Someone focused on fat loss needs a deficit and foods that help manage hunger through that deficit. Someone trying to gain weight might actually need to add calorie-dense foods that a fat-loss client would be told to limit.

GoalWhat Changes
Building muscleHigher protein, calories at maintenance or slight surplus
Fat lossModerate calorie deficit, emphasis on satiating whole foods
Weight gainMore calorie-dense foods added intentionally, like nut butters and oils

It Has to Be Sustainable for Your Life

This is separate from enjoyment. A diet can be enjoyable in theory and still be unsustainable if it doesn't fit how you actually live. Cooking every single meal at home sounds great on paper, but if you have a demanding job and kids with a packed schedule, that plan will collapse the first time life gets busy.

Sustainable means building in flexibility on purpose: two or three home-cooked meals a week instead of seven, a meal prep service for the days you can't cook, room for a dinner out or a slice of cake at a birthday party without it derailing everything. The goal is a system that survives contact with your real life, not just your best week.

Key Takeaways
  • A healthy diet meets your macronutrient and micronutrient needs, so avoid eliminating whole food groups without a medical reason.
  • Manage hunger through a moderate deficit, whole foods, and better meal timing, not extreme restriction.
  • Your diet has to be enjoyable, meaning it uses foods you actually like, not just foods you tolerate.
  • The right diet depends on your specific goal: muscle gain, fat loss, and weight gain all require different adjustments.
  • Sustainability means building in flexibility for your actual lifestyle, not designing a plan that only works on your best days.

If your current approach is missing even one of these five things, that's the gap worth closing, not more willpower or more restriction. A diet that checks all five boxes doesn't need to be perfect to work. It just needs to be something you can keep doing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the 5 things a healthy diet needs to do?

A healthy, sustainable diet needs to meet your nutritional needs, help regulate your hunger and satiety, be enjoyable, support your specific goals, and be sustainable for your actual lifestyle. If any of these are missing, the diet will eventually break down.

Why do restrictive diets like keto or carnivore fail long term?

Restrictive diets eliminate entire food groups, which makes it harder to meet both macronutrient and micronutrient needs, and they're rarely enjoyable or aligned with someone's actual lifestyle. That combination makes them difficult to sustain, even when they produce short-term results.

How big of a calorie deficit is actually sustainable?

A slight to moderate deficit, roughly 10 to 20% below maintenance calories, is generally sustainable for most people. Going far below that makes hunger and satiety much harder to manage, which increases the odds of falling off the diet entirely.

Does a diet have to be perfect to be healthy?

No. A healthy diet is built on consistency, not perfection. What matters far more than any single meal is whether the overall pattern meets your nutritional needs, fits your goals, and is something you can actually keep doing.
