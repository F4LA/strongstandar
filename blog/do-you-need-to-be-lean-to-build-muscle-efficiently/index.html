Do You Need to Be Lean to Build Muscle?

You've heard staying lean makes your calorie surplus more efficient for building muscle. Competitive bodybuilder Steve Hall breaks down why that theory falls apart under real research.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· August 29, 2026 · 7 min read
SH
Steve Hall
Competitive bodybuilder with 10 years on stage, host of the Revive Stronger podcast.
Quick Answer

No, you don't need to stay lean for muscle building to be efficient. The "P-ratio" theory, that leaner people partition more of their surplus calories toward muscle rather than fat, doesn't hold up well against research, including a study on sumo wrestlers showing similar partitioning ratios despite significantly higher body fat.

A common belief in the bodybuilding and fitness world is that staying leaner makes your calorie surplus more "efficient," meaning a higher percentage of those extra calories goes toward building muscle rather than fat. Steve Hall, a competitive bodybuilder with 10 years on stage, spent years restricting himself around this exact fear, before learning the research doesn't actually support it.

The Claim

"Staying leaner improves your P-ratio, meaning more of your surplus calories get partitioned toward muscle instead of fat."

What the Sumo Wrestler Study Actually Found

Steve points to a paper referenced by Mass Research Review that examined sumo wrestlers, a population with body fat percentages over 30%, well above what most people would consider lean. The finding: their P-ratios were just as good as leaner individuals, undermining the idea that leanness itself drives more efficient muscle partitioning.

P-ratio has been debunked. Partitioning ratios are really not something you need to be worried about as a trainee.

This doesn't mean unlimited fat gain is a good strategy, excessive body fat does come with real downsides for well-being, sleep, gym endurance, and recovery. But the specific fear that being slightly leaner meaningfully improves how efficiently your body builds muscle doesn't hold up under this evidence.

The Related Mistake: Obsessing Over High Carbs, Low Fat

Steve also spent a stretch of his career following a high-carb, low-fat approach to mass building, based on the theory that carbohydrates are "anti-catabolic" due to their insulin response, and therefore more optimal for muscle gain than fat.

In practice, he didn't notice the purported muscle-building benefits, while the unnecessary fat restriction made his diet significantly less enjoyable and harder to sustain. He also points out that adequate dietary fat may support testosterone production, a tradeoff the high-carb approach didn't account for.

The Bigger Lesson: Restriction for Its Own Sake Doesn't Pay Off

Both of these mistakes share a common thread: restricting something, body fat percentage or dietary fat, based on a theoretical mechanism that didn't produce a meaningful real-world benefit, while making the overall process harder to sustain.

Steve's current approach focuses on the body composition that supports his best possible training performance, since training itself, not micromanaging leanness or macros, is what actually drives muscle growth.

Key Takeaways
  • The P-ratio theory, that leanness improves how efficiently calories are partitioned toward muscle, isn't well supported by research.
  • A study on sumo wrestlers with over 30% body fat found P-ratios comparable to leaner individuals.
  • A high-carb, low-fat approach based on insulin's "anti-catabolic" effect didn't produce noticeable extra muscle gain in practice.
  • Unnecessary dietary restriction, whether around body fat or specific macros, tends to make a plan less sustainable without a proven payoff.
  • What actually drives muscle growth is high-quality, productive training, not obsessive management of leanness or macro ratios.

Chasing theoretical efficiency gains through extreme leanness or rigid macro ratios often costs more in sustainability than it delivers in results. Training hard and consistently remains the variable that actually matters most.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do you need to stay lean to build muscle efficiently?

No. The "P-ratio" theory, that leaner people partition more calories toward muscle versus fat, doesn't hold up well. A study looking at sumo wrestlers with over 30% body fat found P-ratios just as good as leaner individuals, suggesting body fat percentage isn't the deciding factor.

What is a P-ratio in bodybuilding nutrition?

P-ratio refers to the theoretical proportion of a calorie surplus that gets partitioned toward muscle gain versus fat gain. The commonly held belief is that leaner people have better P-ratios, but research hasn't strongly supported this as a major practical factor.

Does a high-carb, low-fat diet build muscle faster?

Not meaningfully, according to one competitive bodybuilder's experience. The theory that high carb intake is "anti-catabolic" due to insulin response didn't produce noticeable extra muscle gain, while the unnecessary fat restriction made the diet less enjoyable and harder to sustain.

What should you actually focus on for building muscle instead of staying lean?

Focus on body composition that supports your best training performance. What actually drives muscle growth is high-quality, productive training, not obsessively managing leanness or specific macronutrient ratios for their own sake.
