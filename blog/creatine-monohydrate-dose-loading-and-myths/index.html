Creatine Monohydrate, Explained

Creatine is the most studied muscle-building supplement, but the questions never stop: which kind, how much, load or not, cycle or not. Dr. Joey answers all of them in one place.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
·September 11, 2026·8 min read
Quick Answer

Take about 5 grams of creatine monohydrate daily. You don't need a loading phase, you don't need to cycle off it, and the time of day doesn't matter as long as you take it consistently. The weight you may gain in the first month is water inside your muscles, not fat, and the hair loss claim isn't supported by the research.

Creatine is by far the most popular muscle-building supplement, and there are thousands of studies behind it. Still, most people have the same handful of questions before they start. Dr. Joey takes them one at a time.

What Creatine Is and How It Works

Creatine is not a steroid, it's a completely natural substance.

Creatine is a naturally occurring molecule found in muscle tissue, and in steak, poultry, and seafood. Food alone won't get you there, though. You would need roughly 16 servings of steak a day to reach the amounts that benefit performance.

Your muscles fuel hard contractions with ATP, but only a small supply is on hand, and it runs out within seconds of intense effort. Your body also stores creatine as phosphocreatine, which donates a phosphate group to quickly rebuild ATP. Supplementing raises those stores, giving you more ATP during a set, which can mean an extra rep or two at the end. Over time, better performance leads to more muscle growth and strength.

Studies back this up. In one trial, participants lifted four days a week with each muscle trained twice, and the creatine group gained significantly more muscle in the upper and lower body than the placebo group. A 2020 study found greater strength gains on the leg press and chest press after six weeks.

Which Type, How Much, and When

Monohydrate
The most studied and cheapest form. Other forms have no evidence of working better.
3 to 6 g
Typical daily dose of 0.02 to 0.03 grams per pound of body weight. 5 grams works for most people.

HCL, ethyl ester, buffered, and similar versions are mostly marketing. Creatine monohydrate is the form with the research behind it, and it costs less.

Dose can scale with body size. A 150-pound person lands at roughly 3 to 4.5 grams, and a 200-pound person at about 4 to 6 grams. Timing doesn't matter because the benefits build over time. Consistency matters most, so pick a time you'll remember. The one exception is right before training, since some people get an upset stomach from creatine combined with pre-workout ingredients like caffeine and beta-alanine, and caffeine may slightly reduce absorption. If that's how you take it, it's not a big deal.

Common Questions and Myths

Do you need a loading phase? No. Loading means about 20 grams a day for the first week to saturate your muscles faster, but it can cause stomach upset, bloating, and cramping. Taking 5 grams a day fully saturates your muscles in about a month anyway.

Does it cause bloating or weight gain? It causes water retention, and you may gain three to five pounds in the first month. That water sits inside your muscle, which can make muscles look fuller, and creatine doesn't increase water under the skin.

Should you stop during a fat loss phase? No. The added weight is water, not fat, and creatine doesn't block fat loss. It may even help you train harder and hold on to muscle while dieting.

Do you need to cycle off it? No. There is no data showing harm from long-term use, and none showing it works less well over time, so stopping has no clear benefit.

Does it cause hair loss? The myth comes from one old study of football players showing a rise in DHT, a testosterone metabolite linked to hair loss. Follow-up studies didn't find that increase, and no studies show creatine causing hair loss.

As with any supplement, if you have a medical condition or take medications, check with your doctor first.

Key Takeaways
  • Creatine monohydrate is the only form you need, since it has the research and costs the least.
  • About 5 grams a day works for most people, or 0.02 to 0.03 grams per pound of body weight.
  • A loading phase and cycling off are both unnecessary, and the time of day doesn't matter.
  • Early weight gain is water inside your muscles, not fat, so there is no reason to stop during a fat loss phase.
  • The hair loss claim comes from one old study that follow-up research did not replicate.

Creatine rewards consistency more than precision. Pick the monohydrate, take around 5 grams every day, and ignore the loading phases, cycling schedules, and fancy forms.

Frequently Asked Questions

What type of creatine is best?

Creatine monohydrate. It is the most researched form and the cheapest, and there is no scientific evidence that other forms such as HCL or buffered creatine work better.

How much creatine should you take per day?

Most people do well with about 5 grams daily. A weight-based range is 0.02 to 0.03 grams per pound, so roughly 3 to 4.5 grams at 150 pounds and 4 to 6 grams at 200 pounds.

Do you need to do a creatine loading phase?

No. Loading at 20 grams a day can cause stomach upset, bloating, and cramping. Taking 5 grams a day saturates your muscles in about a month.

Does creatine cause hair loss?

The claim traces to one old study showing higher DHT in football players. Follow-up studies did not find that increase, and no studies show creatine causing hair loss.
