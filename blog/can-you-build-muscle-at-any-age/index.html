Can You Build Muscle at Any Age?

You've heard that muscle loss is just an inevitable part of getting older. Joelle Cavagnaro breaks down why that's only half true, using her own 83-year-old grandfather as proof.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· August 19, 2026 · 7 min read
JC
Joelle Cavagnaro
Master's degree in exercise science, pre- and postnatal specialist, and owner of Levolution Coaching.
Quick Answer

No, it's not too late to build muscle, even in your 70s or 80s. Most age-related muscle loss isn't an unavoidable biological decline, it's largely a result of disuse. Muscles that stay active through consistent movement and resistance training can still grow stronger at any age.

Sarcopenia, the medical term for age-related muscle loss, is real. But the way it gets talked about often makes it sound like an inevitable countdown that starts the moment you hit a certain age, with nothing you can do about it. That's not the full picture.

The Claim

"I can't build muscle well, now I'm too old."

Joelle Cavagnaro, who holds a master's degree in exercise science, hears versions of this claim constantly, including recently from her own 83-year-old grandfather.

What Happened When Her Grandfather Stopped Moving

Joelle's grandfather was hospitalized and unable to eat, drink, or get out of bed for several days following surgery. By the time he was cleared to start moving again, he could barely stand or walk on his own, and felt convinced his body was simply giving out on him at 83.

He began intensive physical therapy, two to three sessions a day, to rebuild his ability to perform basic daily tasks: standing, walking, caring for his dog, using the bathroom independently. Discouraged, he asked Joelle directly whether he could actually still build muscle at his age, certain the answer would be no.

Why Muscle Loss Is Usually About Disuse, Not Just Age

Joelle's answer surprised him: yes, he could still gain strength and muscle. While sarcopenia is a real phenomenon, most muscle loss people experience isn't purely a function of aging. It's a consequence of not using the muscles that support movement and daily function.

Most of the time when the issue of muscle loss arises, it is not an inherent muscle loss that's just happening to you because you're 80. It's more so an issue of disuse.

The exercises Joelle gave her grandfather turned out to closely match what his physical therapist had him doing that same day, reinforcing that appropriate resistance training and rehabilitation share the same underlying principle: muscles respond to being used, regardless of age.

Why This Matters Beyond How You Look

Many people start a fitness journey focused primarily on how their body looks. What gets overlooked is how directly muscle and movement connect to broader health outcomes: lower all-cause mortality, reduced cancer risk, better blood sugar regulation, and improved cholesterol levels.

These benefits don't show up in a mirror, which makes them easy to forget. But for someone like Joelle's grandfather, the connection between muscle and independence became immediate and undeniable: the ability to walk his dog and manage basic daily tasks came directly from rebuilding strength he'd assumed was permanently gone.

Key Takeaways
  • Sarcopenia, age-related muscle loss, is real, but it's not the primary driver of most muscle loss people experience.
  • Disuse, not simply getting older, is usually the bigger factor behind reduced strength and muscle mass.
  • Even in your 70s and 80s, appropriate resistance training and movement can rebuild meaningful strength.
  • Muscle supports independence in daily life: standing, walking, and performing basic tasks without assistance.
  • Muscle and movement are linked to lower mortality risk, better blood sugar control, and lower cancer risk, not just appearance.

If you've written off strength training because you assume you're past the point where it matters, that assumption is worth questioning. Muscle responds to being used at nearly any age, and the benefits reach far beyond how you look in the mirror.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it too late to build muscle if you're in your 70s or 80s?

No. Most age-related muscle loss is driven by disuse, not an unavoidable biological decline. People in their 70s and 80s can still gain strength and muscle through consistent, appropriate resistance training and movement.

What is sarcopenia and is it inevitable?

Sarcopenia is age-related muscle loss, and it is a real phenomenon. However, in most cases, the bigger driver of muscle loss isn't simply getting older, it's not using the muscles that support daily movement and function.

Why does muscle matter beyond how you look?

Muscle supports your ability to perform daily activities like standing up, walking, and carrying things, and it's linked to lower all-cause mortality, lower cancer risk, and better blood sugar and cholesterol regulation, benefits that go well beyond appearance.

Why do people lose muscle after being immobile, like a hospital stay?

Extended immobility, like time spent recovering in a hospital bed, causes rapid muscle loss because the muscles simply aren't being used. That loss is largely reversible with consistent movement and structured exercise afterward, even for older adults.
