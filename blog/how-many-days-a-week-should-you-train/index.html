How Many Days a Week Should You Actually Train?

You train hard when life is calm and stop completely the moment it isn't. Strength coach Luka Hocevar explains why that pattern, not your split, is what's keeping you from progress.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· July 27, 2026 · 8 min read
LH
Luka Hocevar
Strength coach with over two decades in the industry, trained by Olympic-level athletes early in his own career. Coaches busy professionals and has mentored some of the most recognized names in online fitness.

You're not skipping workouts out of laziness. You show up, you train hard, and then something in life gets harder, a work crunch, a new baby, a bad stretch, and training goes from four days a week to zero. Weeks later you try to restart at the same intensity you left off at, it doesn't stick, and the cycle repeats.

I talked with strength coach Luka Hocevar, who has spent over two decades training everyone from busy professionals to elite athletes, about why this pattern is so common and what actually breaks it. His answer had almost nothing to do with exercise selection.

The All-or-Nothing Pattern That's Actually Sabotaging You

Hocevar calls this the rest-pause mentality: I'll go hard when life is easier, and when it gets harder, I'll stop completely instead of just dialing it down. He hears the same excuse constantly. I'll get back to it when things get smoother. His response is direct: when do things actually get smoother? For almost everyone, the honest answer is rarely, and life will always have another hard stretch waiting on the other side of this one.

The people who stay consistent long-term aren't the ones with fewer hard stretches. They're the ones who learned to keep training through the hard stretches, just at a lower dial setting instead of at zero.

Why a Longer Runway Beats an Aggressive Start

Most people entering or restarting a training routine do the opposite of what actually works. They download a plan built around a deadline, four days of strength, three days of conditioning, a full nutrition overhaul, all starting Monday. Hocevar compares it to someone throwing you the entire codebase for a new programming language and expecting you to be fluent in it while you still have a job and kids.

Aggressive Start
  • Four strength days, three conditioning days from day one
  • Built around an 8 to 12 week deadline
  • Feels unsustainable within the first two weeks
  • One bad week and the whole plan collapses
Longer Runway
  • Two full body strength sessions plus one cardio session
  • Built around a 6 to 12 month horizon
  • Feels doable enough to actually keep doing
  • A bad week just means one lighter week, not a restart

His reasoning is backed by a principle he picked up from a mentor years ago: people drastically overestimate what they can do in the short term, and just as drastically underestimate what they can do over a year. Two full body sessions a week for six months beats four intense sessions a week that fall apart in month one, every time.

The Dial: How to Adjust Without Quitting

Every physical quality, Hocevar explains, develops through the same underlying pattern: stimulus, recovery, adaptation, stabilization, then repeat. No method skips this and no method makes it faster than the time it takes to actually happen.

1
Stimulus
You expose your body to a training demand, whatever that looks like at your current entry point.
2
Recovery
Your body needs the time between sessions to actually absorb that demand. Skipping this is where burnout starts.
3
Adaptation
Strength, muscle, or conditioning improves as a direct response to the stimulus and recovery cycle.
4
Stabilization
This is the step people skip. You need to hold a new level long enough that it becomes your new normal before adding more.
5
Repeat
Once stabilized, you add the next layer, whether that's another session, more weight, or more conditioning.

This is where the dial comes in. Picture your training and nutrition effort on a scale from zero to ten. When life gets harder, Hocevar's rule is simple: you're allowed to turn the dial down to a four or a five, but you never turn it to zero. When his own daughter was born, his long-standing four day upper lower split dropped to three full body days and roughly one conditioning session a week instead of three. The dial moved down. It didn't switch off.

People overestimate what they can do in the short term, but they drastically underestimate what they can do in the long term.

The Simplest Split That Actually Works for Busy People

When it comes to an actual starting template, Hocevar keeps it deliberately simple. For the busy professional juggling a career and a family, three full body strength sessions a week covers the vast majority of cases, backed by research and by years of coaching real people with real schedules. Lower, upper, full body splits work too, but full body wins on time efficiency for most busy people.

He's equally clear on intensity for beginners: you don't need to chase maximum effort to make progress early on. Roughly half of your one rep max is enough to create real adaptation when you're new to structured training. The demand for higher effort only increases as you advance, which is exactly why a longer runway that starts manageable and builds is the model that lasts.

  • Start with two to three full body strength sessions a week, not four or five
  • Add one conditioning session, and treat a second as a bonus, not a requirement
  • Give any new routine six months before judging whether it's "enough"
  • When life gets harder, lower the dial to a four or five instead of stopping
  • Add a new session or more intensity only after the current level feels stable
Key Takeaways
  • The rest-pause mentality, going hard then stopping completely, does more damage than training at a lower intensity consistently.
  • A longer runway, built around months instead of weeks, produces better results than an aggressive short-term plan.
  • Every physical adaptation follows stimulus, recovery, adaptation, stabilization, repeat. Skipping stabilization is a common mistake.
  • Think of effort as a dial from zero to ten. You can lower it when life gets hard, but never turn it off.
  • Three full body strength sessions a week is enough of a template for most busy professionals to build real progress.

The question was never really how many days a week you should train. It's whether the plan you picked can survive an actually hard month, because you will have one. Build a routine you can dial down instead of one you have to abandon, and the days per week take care of themselves.
