How Many Days a Week Should You Actually Train?

You train hard when life is calm and stop completely the moment it isn't. Strength coach Luka Hocevar explains why that pattern, not your split, is what's keeping you from progress.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· July 27, 2026 · 9 min read
LH
Luka Hocevar
Strength coach with over two decades in the industry, has personally coached more than 12,000 people in person across two gyms. Trained by Olympic-level athletes early in his own career, now coaches busy professionals and elite athletes alike.
Quick Answer

Most busy professionals only need three full body strength sessions a week. Progress stalls far more often from sets that aren't close enough to failure than from too few training days. Fix intensity first, then arrange those three days so hard efforts never stack back to back.

You've been training five, sometimes six days a week for months. You're not skipping sessions, you're not slacking off, and the number on the bar hasn't moved in longer than you'd like to admit. So you do what most people do next: add a day. Add volume. Push harder on the days you're already pushing hard. And somehow you still end up more tired and no stronger.

I sat down with strength coach Luka Hocevar, who has personally coached over 12,000 people, to figure out why this keeps happening. What he told me flips the usual assumption on its head: the plateau almost never comes from too little training. It comes from training that isn't hard enough, arranged in a way you can't recover from.

Why More Days Isn't the Fix, Intensity Is

The Assumption Almost Everyone Makes

When progress stalls, the instinct is to add. Add a day, add sets, add an extra exercise for the lagging body part. It feels productive, and it feels like the responsible thing to do when you're not seeing results. Hocevar says this instinct is almost always wrong, and it's the single most common mistake he sees in people who've already been training for years.

What's Actually Happening Instead

After 20 years of coaching, there's one thing Hocevar sees almost every single time someone comes to him stuck: their intensity is too low, not their volume.

He walked me through an example. A client who'd been training for years worked up to a three-rep trap bar deadlift and stopped at 215 pounds, convinced that was close to his limit. Hocevar said nothing and wrote it down. Later, on dumbbell bench press, he asked the client to pick a weight he could do for 10 reps. The client hit 10 with no involuntary slowdown at all, meaning he clearly had more left. Hocevar pushed him to keep going. The 10 reps turned into 23.

One Client, Same Program, Higher Intensity
Starting 3-rep trap bar deadlift215 lbs
16 weeks later365 lbs for 4 reps
One year later425 lbs for a double

That's 210 pounds added to the lift in a year, with fewer wasted sets and, in the client's own words, sessions that took less time than what he was doing before. Nothing about his schedule changed. What changed was how close to failure each working set actually got. Hocevar calls the wasted, half-effort sets "junk volume," and it's the default mode for most people who've been training a while without a coach pushing them.

So if the fix is pushing harder, the obvious next question is why not just do that on every single day you train. Which is exactly the mistake Hocevar sees people make next.

Why Training Harder on More Days Backfires

The stimulus you create in a workout only counts if your body can actually recover from it. Push hard, don't recover, and you hit a wall: fatigue climbs, performance drops, and the extra days you added stop paying off.

Hocevar has seen this exact pattern with long-time clients who come in doing five days a week of strength training and getting nowhere. His fix sounds counterintuitive until you see it play out: cut it to three days, arranged deliberately, like Monday, Thursday, Saturday. Clients argue with him at first. Three months later, they tell him it's the best progress they've made in years, because the harder sets on those three days finally have room to be absorbed instead of stacked on top of unrecovered fatigue.

This is where the specific arrangement of those three days starts to matter as much as the number itself.

The Weekly Rhythm That Makes Recovery Possible

Hocevar uses a simple color system, borrowed from sprint coach Charlie Francis, to plan a week: red days are your hardest, highest-effort sessions. Green days are medium. Blue days are true recovery, mobility or light aerobic work. The rule is simple. Never stack two red days back to back.

Common Mistake
  • Hard heavy squat day, then hard conditioning the next day
  • Two red days stacked back to back
  • No day fully recovers before the next hard effort
  • Fatigue accumulates until performance drops
The Rhythm That Works
  • Red, blue, green, red, blue, or similar spacing
  • A lower body heavy day and an upper body heavy day count differently
  • Each hard session gets real recovery before the next one
  • Intensity can stay high because fatigue never stacks

The type of lift matters here too. A heavy trap bar deadlift taxes your whole nervous system and takes days to recover from. A heavy bench press the next day, by comparison, barely touches your legs or lower back, so it can effectively function as a lighter, "green" day even at real intensity. Once your week is arranged this way, three well-placed hard days can produce more than five days that never let you recover enough to push hard in the first place.

The stimulus you create, you have to be able to recover from. If you don't, you're going to run into a wall.

What Actually Happens Inside One of Those Sessions

None of this works if the sessions themselves are unfocused, and Hocevar runs every single one, whether it's a beginner or an elite athlete, through the same seven-part structure he borrowed and adapted from coach Mike Robertson, the R7 method. It's built so a full, effective session fits inside about an hour.

1
Release
Five minutes of soft tissue work: foam rolling, lacrosse ball, whatever loosens up tight tissue before you move.
2
Reset
A minute or two of positional breathing to bring a stressed nervous system down before asking it to perform.
3
Readiness
Dynamic warm-ups specific to what you're about to lift: hip openers and ankle work before a squat, for example.
4
Reactive
A small dose of power work, a box jump or a med ball throw. Power is the first physical quality people lose with age, so it's trained every session, not just for athletes.
5
Resistance
Your actual strength and muscle-building work. This is where the intensity from earlier in this article gets applied.
6
Resilience
Conditioning, placed after strength work when time allows, following the same red-day logic covered above.
7
Recovery
A short cool-down to close the session out before you leave the gym.

Notice what's flexible inside that structure and what isn't. Hocevar is firm that movement patterns are mandatory, a squat pattern, a hinge, a push, a pull, but the specific exercise is not. A goblet squat, a pendulum squat, and a barbell back squat all satisfy the same pattern, and he picks whichever one fits the person's body and experience in front of him. That flexibility is exactly what makes this structure usable for a beginner and an elite athlete on the same afternoon.

Fitting This Into a Life That Doesn't Cooperate

Here's the honest catch: none of the above matters if the plan collapses the first time life gets hard, a work crunch, a new baby, a bad stretch. Hocevar has lived this personally. When his daughter was born, his long-standing four day upper-lower split dropped to three full body days and about one conditioning session a week instead of three.

He frames effort on a dial from zero to ten. You're allowed to turn it down to a four or five when life demands it. You are never allowed to turn it to zero. For clients whose schedules genuinely can't support three sessions, nurses working odd shifts, for example, he uses a model he calls two-plus-two-for-life: two strength sessions and two conditioning sessions a week, built to be sustainable indefinitely rather than powered through for eight weeks and abandoned.

That's the same principle behind his preference for a longer runway over an aggressive start. People consistently overestimate what they can do in the next few weeks and underestimate what they can build over the next year. Two honest, high-intensity full body sessions a week for six months beats four sessions a week that collapse in month one, every time.

Key Takeaways
  • A plateau usually means your sets aren't close enough to failure, not that you're not training enough days. Push the intensity before you add volume.
  • The stimulus you create has to match what you can actually recover from. More hard days without recovery makes you weaker, not stronger.
  • Arrange your week so hard days never stack back to back. A heavy squat day and a heavy bench day can sit next to each other because they tax different systems.
  • Every session should move through release, reset, readiness, power, strength, conditioning, and cool-down, in about an hour.
  • Keep a dial from zero to ten for hard weeks. Turn it down to a four or five if you have to. Never turn it to zero.

The real question was never how many days a week you should train. It's whether the days you do train are hard enough to matter, spaced out enough to recover from, and built to survive the month your life actually throws at you. Get those three things right, and three well-placed sessions will out-produce five scattered ones every time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it bad to strength train the same muscle group every day?

It depends on the intensity and the muscle group. Training a muscle heavy every single day without recovery is how plateaus happen, since the stimulus never gets absorbed before the next session. Spacing hard sessions for the same muscle group at least a day apart, or alternating a heavy lower body day with a heavy upper body day, lets you train frequently without stalling.

How do I know if my training intensity is actually high enough?

Ask whether your last set has an honest, involuntary slowdown, meaning the bar visibly speeds down or you genuinely could not do another clean rep. If you consistently stop sets with several reps left in the tank, that's the first thing to fix before adding more days or more volume.

What should I do on weeks when I barely have time to train?

Turn the effort dial down instead of stopping completely. Two shorter full body sessions instead of three, or a lighter version of your normal session, keeps the habit and the adaptation alive. Going to zero is what actually costs you the progress you already built.

Do I need more than an hour in the gym per session?

For most people, no. A full session covering warm-up, a short power exercise, your main strength work, and conditioning fits inside about an hour when it's structured well. Sessions that run much longer are usually a sign of too much rest between sets or too many low-value exercises, not a sign of a better workout.
