Why You Can't Keep the Weight Off

You've lost 40, 50, even 60 lbs before, more than once. If the weight keeps coming back, the problem was never your plan. Here's what actually needs to change.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· July 21, 2026 · 6 min read

You've done this before. Maybe more than once. You've lost 20, 40, even 60 lbs, put in the discipline for months, and gotten yourself to a place where you felt good. And then, slowly, it came back. A few pounds turn into ten, ten turns into twenty, and eventually you're right back where you started, or worse.

If this sounds familiar, I want to tell you something I've learned after working with over a thousand clients: the problem almost never is the plan. You know how to lose weight, you've proven that already. The reason it doesn't stick is something deeper, and it's rarely discussed in the way it needs to be.

The Goal That Isn't Strong Enough to Hold

I had a conversation recently with a friend in his 40s. Successful business, great marriage, two young daughters, admired in his community, everything most people would call figured out. Except his weight. He's lost 40, 50, 60 lbs multiple times in his life and gained it all back every single time.

I asked him why. He told me that when he loses the weight, he starts to loosen up, slip back into old habits, and gain 5 lbs that "doesn't mean anything." Five turns into ten, ten into twenty, and he's back to the point where he feels uncomfortable enough to start over. His honest words were that being a little leaner doesn't really mean much to him, and his wife loves him either way.

He doesn't have a strong enough why. Or at least he's never made it conscious. That's the exact reason the weight keeps coming back.

When he said that, it was a genuine lightbulb moment for both of us. He wasn't lacking discipline. He was chasing a goal that wasn't rooted in anything deep enough to survive the moment life got hard or boring.

An Outcome Goal Will Always Lose to Real Life

"I want to lose 20 lbs" is not a bad goal, but it's not a why, it's an outcome. And outcome-based goals are inherently self-centered: they're about how you look, not about who you are or what you stand for. When you hit the number, there's nothing left pulling you forward, so you drift.

Fragile
Outcome-Based Why
"I want to lose 20 lbs." Self-focused, finish-line thinking. Once hit, or once life gets stressful, motivation disappears.
Durable
Identity-Based Why
"I want to be the parent who's still active at my kid's wedding." Rooted in who you are, not a number. Survives stress, setbacks, and boredom.

I've seen this play out in my own life. A few months ago I was navigating my wife's postpartum anxiety, a rough financial stretch in my business, the loss of my aunt, and trying to rebuild a relationship with my mom, all at once. I still didn't miss a single one of my three weekly workouts. Not because my goal was a number on the scale, but because staying strong and present is part of who I am, not something I'm chasing.

Three Questions That Get to the Real Answer

Most people have never consciously sat down and answered why any of this actually matters to them. It's sitting in the subconscious, unexamined. So here are the three questions I ask clients when we have this conversation, and I want you to actually sit with them, not answer them in ten seconds.

1
If nothing changes, what does your life look like in 10 or 20 years?
Be honest, not dramatic. For most people stuck in the yo-yo cycle, this looks like more of the same: repeated regain, and the health consequences that come with carrying excess weight for decades.
2
Who pays the price for that, besides you?
Your kids, your spouse, the people who look up to you. It's rarely just you who's affected by where this goes, and that reframes the stakes considerably.
3
What version of you would make the people you love proud?
What does that version of you do every single day? Get specific. This is the identity you're actually building toward, not the number on the scale.

Write the answers down, in detail. This isn't a five-minute exercise if you take it seriously, and it shouldn't be. Once it's etched into how you think about yourself, it becomes the thing your brain reaches for in the moment you want to skip a workout or say the diet doesn't matter anymore.

Key Takeaways
  • If you've lost the same weight more than once, the plan isn't the problem, the why underneath it is.
  • Outcome-based goals like "lose 20 lbs" are self-focused and don't survive stress or boredom.
  • Identity-based whys, tied to who you want to be for the people you love, hold up when life gets hard.
  • Ask yourself: what happens in 10 years if nothing changes, who pays the price, and what version of you would make your family proud.
  • Write your answers down. A why that's only ever been subconscious won't be there for you in the moment you need it.

Strength isn't the goal here, it's the standard, and it's the vehicle for becoming stronger in every area of your life, not just your body. Get honest and specific about your why, and the plan you already know how to execute finally has something strong enough to hold onto.
