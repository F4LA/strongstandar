Why Extreme Content Goes Viral

"Never eat seed oils again." "Oats are killing you." Coach Harry Thorne breaks down why nutrition content this extreme spreads so fast, and why nuanced advice barely gets seen.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· September 3, 2026 · 7 min read
HT
Harry Thorne
Online fitness and nutrition coach focused on countering social media misinformation with balanced, evidence-based content.
Quick Answer

Extreme nutrition claims like "never eat seed oils" or "oats will kill you" spread faster because bold, absolute statements act as effective attention-grabbing hooks on social media, while nuanced, accurate explanations struggle to compete for views. Health and nutrition are mostly nuance, which doesn't fit the format that performs best.

If you've scrolled through health content lately, you've seen the pattern: "never eat this again," "this common food is secretly poisoning you." Harry Thorne, an online coach who focuses on countering misinformation, breaks down exactly why this style of content spreads so much faster than balanced, accurate advice.

The "Never Eat Seed Oils" Example

The Viral Claim

"Never eat seed oils again, they're poisoning you."

Harry points out that this isn't really about the underlying advice being entirely wrong, moderating certain oils can be reasonable general guidance. The problem is the delivery: extreme, absolute framing that treats a nuanced topic like a black-and-white emergency.

It's not about what they're saying, it's how they're communicating it. And it's because of this communication that people get so terrified.

Why Scary Technical Words Make Claims More Convincing

Harry uses another example: claiming oats will hurt you because of glyphosate, phytic acid, and oxalates. To someone who understands nutrition science, these compounds aren't a significant real-world concern at typical dietary levels. But to the average person who's never heard those words, they sound alarming purely because they're unfamiliar and technical-sounding.

Why This Content Format Wins

Content creators need attention to succeed, and bold, absolute statements consistently outperform nuanced explanations for generating views. A calm, detailed breakdown of a nutrition topic might get a fraction of the engagement compared to a dramatic, oversimplified claim about the same subject.

Health is not hooks. Health is not bold statements. Health and nutrition is all nuance, but the content that does best has got the biggest hooks.

This creates a frustrating cycle for anyone trying to communicate accurate nutrition information: the audience is conditioned by algorithms to want dramatic hard facts, but genuine health and nutrition science rarely offers hard facts in that simplified form, since so much depends on total diet, dose, and individual context.

What This Means for You as a Consumer

Recognizing this pattern is the first defense against being pulled in by it. When you see a claim framed as an absolute, "never eat this," "this one food is destroying your health," treat that extreme framing itself as a signal to look closer, rather than a sign the claim must be especially important or urgent.

Key Takeaways
  • Extreme, absolute nutrition claims spread faster because they function as effective attention-grabbing hooks.
  • The delivery of a claim, not necessarily the underlying advice, is often what makes it misleading or alarming.
  • Unfamiliar, technical-sounding chemical names make claims feel more convincing than they actually are.
  • Nuanced, accurate nutrition content consistently underperforms bold, oversimplified claims on social media.
  • Treating extreme, absolute framing as a red flag, rather than a sign of importance, is a useful defense against misinformation.

The most viral nutrition content isn't necessarily the most accurate. Understanding why extreme framing spreads so effectively is one of the most useful tools for filtering what's actually worth taking seriously.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are seed oils actually dangerous to eat?

The blanket claim that seed oils are poisoning you is an extreme oversimplification not well supported by the evidence. Extreme framing like this is common in viral content because it generates far more attention than a nuanced explanation would.

Why does extreme nutrition content get more views than nuanced content?

Bold, absolute claims act as attention-grabbing hooks, which perform far better on social media than careful, nuanced explanations. Since content creators need views to succeed, this creates pressure toward oversimplified, alarming claims rather than accurate ones.

Why do scary-sounding chemical names make nutrition claims feel more convincing?

Unfamiliar, technical-sounding words like glyphosate or phytic acid sound alarming to people who don't understand what they are or how they behave in the body, even when the actual risk from typical exposure is minimal or well understood by nutrition science.

Why is health and nutrition mostly nuance rather than hard facts?

Very few claims in nutrition science are absolute across every person and context. Most guidance depends on total diet, individual factors, and dose, which doesn't fit neatly into the short, bold statements that tend to perform best on social media.
