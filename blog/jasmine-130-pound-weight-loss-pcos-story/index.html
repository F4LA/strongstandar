Jasmine's 130 Pound Weight Loss Story

Jasmine Luna was diagnosed with PCOS at 15 and reached her heaviest weight at 325 pounds. She sat down with Dr. Joey to share what actually changed things, and it wasn't any single tool.

Quick Answer

Jasmine's biggest change wasn't a specific diet, medication, or procedure. It was a shift in mindset: treating consistency as an ongoing pattern rather than a perfect streak, and learning that a missed workout or untracked meal isn't a failure, just a data point to build from.

Jasmine Luna was diagnosed with PCOS at 15 years old. Along with it came hormonal imbalances, insulin resistance, and weight gain that followed her for over a decade. By the time she reached her heaviest, she was at 325 pounds, managing high blood pressure with medication and feeling like her health was slipping further out of her control.

Today, she's at 194 pounds, a full-time nursing student, and someone who describes herself as a genuinely different person, not just physically. I sat down with her to talk about what that road actually looked like, because it wasn't a straight line, and it wasn't about any single tool doing all the work.

A Long Road Before Things Started to Shift

For years, Jasmine's experience with PCOS meant being handed medical options, birth control, metformin, that helped with some symptoms but never really addressed the weight itself. She was young, uncertain about being on medication long-term, and stuck in a cycle that felt largely outside her control.

Eventually, rising blood pressure and the prospect of long-term medication became the moment that pushed her to look for something different. She worked with her medical team on options along the way, including a period of GLP-1 medication that helped her make initial progress, but she found the side effects difficult and made the decision, with her doctor, to stop.

What Actually Started to Change Things

Jasmine had avoided the gym for years, partly out of a misconception that lifting weights as a larger person would just make her bigger and bulkier. She leaned on cardio instead, without much result. It wasn't until she started structured coaching that her actual daily habits, tracking food, going to the gym consistently, began to shift in a way cardio alone never had.

In the months before a bariatric surgery she'd already decided to pursue, working through consistent nutrition and training brought her down from just over 300 pounds to 284, progress she says had never happened for her on her own. That period taught her the daily habits that carried forward long after the surgery itself.

The Mindset Shift That Mattered More Than Any Tool

Jasmine described a specific turning point that had nothing to do with food or exercise directly: changing how she talked to herself when she missed a workout or a day of tracking. She used to treat any gap as the start of losing her consistency entirely.

It's okay if you don't go to the gym, it's okay if you don't meal prep today. It's an ongoing progress.

That reframe, treating a missed day as a normal part of an ongoing process rather than proof she was failing, is what let her keep going instead of spiraling after a setback. She also leaned on a simple, flexible approach to food: "eat what you want, add what you need," meaning she didn't eliminate meals she enjoyed, she just built in the protein or vegetables that were missing.

Why Tracking Still Matters to Her

Even now, Jasmine tracks her food and her workouts, not out of obsession, but because it gives her an honest picture of where she actually stands. She compares it to tracking spending to manage money, or tracking performance to improve at work: it's simply part of the process for anything that's truly a priority.

She's also stepped back from weighing herself daily, choosing instead to check in periodically and celebrate the smaller wins along the way, like seeing a number she hadn't seen since high school.

Key Takeaways
  • Jasmine's health history with PCOS included hormonal imbalances and weight gain starting in her mid-teens.
  • Structured coaching, not cardio alone, is what finally moved her weight in the months leading up to her surgery.
  • The real turning point was a mindset shift: treating a missed day as normal, not as proof of failure.
  • "Eat what you want, add what you need" let her keep enjoying food while still meeting her nutritional needs.
  • She still tracks food and training today, viewing it as an honest tool for staying aligned with her goals, not a punishment.

Jasmine's story isn't about one decision that changed everything. It's a reminder that lasting change is usually built from a lot of ordinary days, and the willingness to keep going even on the days that don't go as planned.

Frequently Asked Questions

What made the biggest difference in Jasmine's weight loss journey?

Jasmine points to a mindset shift as the turning point, not any single tool. She stopped treating a missed workout or an untracked meal as a failure, and started treating consistency as an ongoing pattern rather than a perfect streak that could be broken.

Does PCOS make weight loss harder?

PCOS can contribute to hormonal imbalances, insulin resistance, and weight gain, which is what Jasmine experienced starting in her mid-teens. That said, her story shows meaningful progress is still achievable with consistent nutrition and training support, alongside guidance from her own medical team.

What does "eat what you want, add what you need" actually mean?

It means you don't have to eliminate foods you enjoy. Instead, you build in what's missing, like adding a serving of vegetables or extra protein to a meal you already wanted to eat, rather than treating that meal as off-limits entirely.

Why does tracking food and workouts matter for long-term progress?

Tracking gives you an honest, ongoing picture of your actual behavior, similar to tracking spending to manage money or tracking performance to improve at a job. Without it, it's much harder to know what's actually working and what needs to change.
