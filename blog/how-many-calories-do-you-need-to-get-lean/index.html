How Many Calories Do You Need to Get Lean?

You know you need to eat less than you burn, but not how much less. Dr. Joey walks through estimating your maintenance calories, picking a deficit, and adjusting as the weight comes off.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
·September 13, 2026·8 min read
Quick Answer

Estimate your basal metabolic rate with a predictive equation, multiply it by an activity factor from 1.2 to 1.9 to get maintenance calories, then subtract 10 to 20 percent to lose weight. Most people should start around 15 percent. Treat the result as a starting point, track your weekly average weight, and reduce calories by about 150 as your weight drops.

Weight loss comes down to energy balance: you need to consume fewer calories than your body expends. To pick a calorie target, you first need a rough idea of how much energy you burn in a day, which is your total daily energy expenditure, or TDEE.

What Makes Up Your Daily Energy Burn

TDEE has four parts, and every calorie you burn falls into one of them.

BMR and NEAT

Your basal metabolic rate is the energy needed to keep you alive and is about 60 percent of the total. Non-exercise activity thermogenesis covers everything outside workouts, from walking around to sitting upright.

Exercise and digestion

Exercise activity thermogenesis is the energy used in deliberate workouts. The thermic effect of food is the energy needed to digest and absorb what you eat.

Measuring each part separately isn't practical, so we use predictive equations to estimate BMR and build from there.

Step One: Estimate Your BMR

The only direct way to measure BMR is calorimetry, which most people can't access. Equations using your age, weight, and height do a good job instead. Plenty of free online BMR calculators apply one of these equations for you, so there's no need to memorize it.

As an example, Dr. Joey is a 220-pound, 28-year-old man at 6 foot 5, and his estimated BMR is about 2,000 calories per day.

Step Two: Multiply by an Activity Factor

1.2
Completely sedentary, the low end of the activity factors
1.9
Extremely active, such as a professional athlete, the high end

Pick the factor that matches your real activity level. Someone who lifts twice a week and runs twice for 30 minutes but has a very sedentary job is lightly active, with a factor of 1.375. Multiplying a BMR of 2,000 by 1.375 gives about 2,750 calories, which is the estimated maintenance level. If you fall between two levels, choose a value in between, for example somewhere from 1.375 to 1.55.

Step Three: Adjust for Your Goal

1
Weight loss: cut 10, 15, or 20 percent
Slow, moderate, and fast pace, in that order. Most people should begin with the moderate 15 percent.
2
Weight gain: add 5, 10, or 15 percent
Slow, moderate, and fast pace. Slow to moderate is better, since faster gain doesn't mean more muscle.
3
Example
A 15 percent cut from 2,750 calories lands at roughly 2,300 calories per day.

The fast option is tempting, but it isn't right for everyone. A larger deficit makes sticking to your diet harder, and you'll likely feel hungrier, lower on energy, and grumpier throughout the day.

Treat the Number as a Starting Point

The numbers generated from these equations are simply predictions.

Your real maintenance could differ from the estimate. The most accurate way to find it is to track your calories for two to three weeks and see whether your weight holds steady. If it does, your intake is close to maintenance.

Track and Adjust Over Time

Track weekly or biweekly averages of your body weight instead of daily readings, since daily weight fluctuates. Losing roughly 0.4 to 0.8 percent of your body weight per week is a good place to be. As you lose weight, your BMR and total expenditure fall, so you'll need to adjust. Cutting about 150 more calories per day is typically enough to keep the rate of loss similar.

Key Takeaways
  • Your TDEE is made up of BMR, non-exercise activity, exercise, and the thermic effect of food, with BMR about 60 percent of the total.
  • Estimate BMR with a predictive equation, then multiply by an activity factor between 1.2 and 1.9.
  • For weight loss, subtract 10 to 20 percent from maintenance, and start at about 15 percent if unsure.
  • These numbers are estimates, and tracking for two to three weeks shows your true maintenance.
  • Aim for 0.4 to 0.8 percent of body weight lost per week, and cut about 150 more calories as your weight falls.

A calculator gets you into the right range, and your own weekly data gets you the rest of the way. Start with a moderate deficit and adjust only when the trend tells you to.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you calculate how many calories you need to lose weight?

Estimate your BMR with a predictive equation, multiply it by an activity factor from 1.2 to 1.9 to get maintenance calories, then reduce that by 10 to 20 percent depending on how fast you want to lose weight.

What deficit should most people start with?

Most people do well starting with a moderate deficit of about 15 percent below maintenance. A 20 percent deficit is faster but tends to leave you hungrier, lower on energy, and harder to stick with.

How accurate are calorie calculators?

They are predictions, not exact measures. Your real maintenance can differ, so track your calories for two to three weeks and see where your weight stays stable.

How fast should you lose weight?

A rate of roughly 0.4 to 0.8 percent of your body weight per week is a good range. As you lose weight, you may need to cut about 150 more calories to keep the same pace.
