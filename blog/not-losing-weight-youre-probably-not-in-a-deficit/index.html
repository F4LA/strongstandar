You're Probably Not in a Deficit

You're tracking 1,500 calories and the scale isn't moving. Coach Jordan Lips explains why that combination is a contradiction, and one simple check to find out what's really going on.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· September 6, 2026 · 7 min read
JL
Jordan Lips
Certified nutritionist and personal trainer, coaching clients on sustainable body composition and lifestyle change.
Quick Answer

Being in a genuine calorie deficit and not losing weight are contradictory, they can't both be true. If you're tracking a deficit but not seeing results, the most likely explanation is unaccounted calories in your tracking, not a broken metabolism. A simple check: if you're not feeling a little hungry by the end of most days, you're probably not actually in a deficit.

"I'm eating 1,500 calories and I'm not losing weight" is one of the most common complaints coach Jordan Lips hears. His response cuts right to the core of the issue: those two facts, a real deficit and no weight loss, are an oxymoron. They can't both be true at the same time.

Why "Deficit But Not Losing Weight" Doesn't Add Up

If someone's like, hey, I'm eating this and I'm not losing weight, we need to start from operating with an understanding that you're not actually in a deficit.

From there, Jordan says there are really only two productive directions: investigate why you're not actually in a deficit despite what your tracker says, or accept that you're not currently in a deficit and adjust your intake going forward. Both are valid. Continuing to insist the math must be wrong while nothing changes is not.

The Simple Hunger Check

Jordan uses a practical gut-check with clients: do you feel a little hungry by the end of most days? If someone eating in a genuine deficit builds their diet to be reasonably satiating, they should still feel a bit hungry between meals and by day's end. That's normal and expected.

The Red Flag

"I'm tracking 1,400 calories, training 4-5 days a week, walking 10,000 steps, and I'm never hungry."

If that describes you, Jordan's blunt conclusion is simple: you're not actually eating 1,400 calories. The math of activity level plus complete satisfaction at a very low intake typically doesn't add up.

Where the Tracking Gap Usually Comes From

Dr. Joey adds that a lot of this comes down to people unintentionally introducing error into their tracking, eyeballing portions, forgetting cooking oil, underestimating snacks or drinks. You don't need to be perfect with tracking, but consistent small errors compound over time into the difference between a real deficit and an accidental maintenance intake.

A Note on Comparing Numbers to Other People

Jordan also cautions against comparing your calorie target to someone else's, even someone your same size. He references a well-known example from sports scientists discussing reverse dieting: a 220-pound, fairly active man eating 1,800 calories and still losing about half a pound a week, roughly consistent with a modest calorie deficit for his size and activity level. What matters isn't comparing that number to your own, it's whether your own numbers and how you feel actually check out together.

Key Takeaways
  • A genuine calorie deficit and a completely stalled scale are contradictory, one of the two isn't accurate.
  • Feeling a bit hungry by the end of most days is a normal sign you're actually in a deficit.
  • Never feeling hungry on a low calorie target at a high activity level is a red flag for tracking inaccuracy.
  • Small, consistent tracking errors, like eyeballing portions or forgetting oil, add up to meaningful gaps over time.
  • Comparing your calorie target to someone else's number is less useful than checking whether your own numbers and hunger levels align.

If you're stuck on the "I'm in a deficit but not losing weight" loop, the hunger check is a fast, practical way to find out what's really going on, without needing to blame your metabolism for a math problem that's usually solvable with a closer look at your tracking.

Frequently Asked Questions

If I'm tracking calories and not losing weight, am I really in a deficit?

Probably not. A genuine calorie deficit and a stalled scale can't both be true for long. If the scale isn't moving despite tracking a deficit, the most likely explanation is unaccounted calories, not a broken metabolism.

How can hunger tell you whether you're actually in a calorie deficit?

If you're eating in a genuine deficit, you should feel a bit hungry by the end of most days, even with a fairly satiating diet. If you feel fully satisfied all day on a low calorie target, it's a sign your actual intake may be higher than what's logged.

Why do people introduce errors into their calorie tracking without realizing it?

Small inaccuracies, like eyeballing portions, forgetting cooking oil, or underestimating snacks, add up over time. You don't need to be perfect with tracking, but consistent small errors can be the difference between a real deficit and an unintentional maintenance intake.

What should you do if you find out you're not actually in a deficit?

You can either investigate where the tracking gap is coming from to correct it, or accept you're not currently in a deficit and adjust your intake going forward. Both are valid, but starting from an honest premise is more productive than continuing to assume the math must be wrong.
