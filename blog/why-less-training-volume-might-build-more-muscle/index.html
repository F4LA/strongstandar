Why Less Volume Might Build More Muscle

You've heard you need 10 to 20 sets per muscle per week. Dr. Chester Sokolowski, one of the most muscular people Dr. Joey knows, trains with just 1 to 4 sets. Here's his reasoning.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· September 8, 2026 · 8 min read

Dr. Chester Sokolowski
PhD in exercise physiology, avid bodybuilder, and university instructor specializing in strength, biomechanics, and hypertrophy training.
Quick Answer

Dr. Chester Sokolowski, one of the most muscular people Dr. Joey knows, trains most muscle groups with just 1 to 4 total sets per week. His reasoning: the widely cited 10-20 sets per muscle per week volume landmark isn't necessarily accurate, and strength progression, not hitting a specific set count, should be the real guide for whether your training volume is working.

Most hypertrophy advice centers on hitting a specific volume landmark, often cited as 10 to 20 sets per muscle per week. Dr. Chester Sokolowski, a PhD in exercise physiology and one of the most muscular people Dr. Joey knows personally, trains with dramatically less, and makes a compelling case for why.

What His Actual Training Looks Like

Chester trains most muscle groups with just 1 to 4 total sets per week. For quads, that might mean one set of squats, one set of leg press, and one set of leg extensions, three total sets, and he's done. For biceps, that could mean two sets on Monday and two sets on Friday, four sets total for the week.

I think we had some original volume landmarks where it's like, you got to do 20 sets a week, and seemingly people are stuck on that. You don't have to do all that volume to maximize muscle growth.

Why Strength Progression Is the Real Signal

Chester's core principle: about 80% of a muscle cell's volume comes from contractile proteins, the same proteins responsible for generating force. Since size and strength are closely related, consistently getting stronger, whether through more reps or more weight, is a reliable practical sign that your training volume and intensity are working, regardless of how many total sets you're doing.

A Simple Way to Find Your Own Optimal Volume

1
Simplify to one repeatable workout
Pick a specific workout, say Monday's session, and repeat it consistently every few days.
2
Track whether you're getting stronger
More reps at the same weight, or more total weight for the same reps, both count as progress.
3
If you're not progressing, reduce volume
A lack of consistent strength progression suggests you're doing too much volume, not too little intensity.
4
Once progressing consistently, add variety
Introduce a second workout variation and cycle between them once your baseline volume is dialed in.

Overtraining Is About Volume, Not Failure

Chester specifically argues that overtraining is more closely associated with doing too much volume than with training too close to failure. If someone isn't progressing, his recommendation is to reduce the number of sets first, rather than assuming the fix is to train further from failure.

Key Takeaways
  • Widely cited volume landmarks like 10-20 sets per muscle per week aren't a strict requirement for everyone to build muscle.
  • Some experienced lifters train effectively with as few as 1 to 4 total sets per muscle per week.
  • Consistent strength progression, not hitting a specific set count, is the real practical indicator that your training is working.
  • If you're not getting stronger on a repeated workout, reducing volume, not necessarily backing off intensity, is often the fix.
  • Overtraining is more closely linked to excessive volume than to training close to true muscular failure.

If you've been chasing a specific volume number without checking whether you're actually getting stronger, it may be time to flip the script: let your strength progression, not a landmark number, tell you how much volume you actually need.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do you need 10-20 sets per muscle per week to build muscle?

Not necessarily. While that range is a commonly cited volume landmark, some experienced lifters and coaches train effectively with as few as 1 to 4 sets per muscle per week, as long as training intensity and strength progression remain high.

How can you tell if your training volume is too high?

If you repeat the same workout every few days and you're not getting stronger, either doing more reps at the same weight or more total weight, that's a sign your volume may be too high relative to your recovery capacity.

What actually causes overtraining, too much volume or training too close to failure?

According to this perspective, overtraining is more closely associated with excessive training volume than with training close to failure. Reducing the number of sets, rather than backing off intensity, is often the more effective fix.

Why does strength progression matter so much for muscle growth?

About 80% of a muscle cell's volume comes from contractile proteins, the same proteins responsible for generating force. Since size and strength are closely related, consistently getting stronger over time is a strong practical indicator that your training is producing muscle growth.
