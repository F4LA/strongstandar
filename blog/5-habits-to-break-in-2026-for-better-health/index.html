5 Habits to Break in 2026

You've tried to quit these before and it never stuck. Here's why willpower alone was never going to work, and the exact replacement for each habit that actually holds.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· July 29, 2026 · 5 min read
Quick Answer

The five habits worth replacing this year are skipping breakfast, doom scrolling at night, sitting for most of the day, avoiding strength training, and mindless late-night snacking. Each one sticks around because it completes a cue-behavior-reward loop, so lasting change means substituting the behavior, not just trying to delete it.

You've tried to quit one of these before. Maybe more than one. You told yourself you'd stop, and for a few days it worked, and then you were right back where you started. If that pattern feels familiar, the problem was never your willpower.

Why "Just Stop" Never Works

The Assumption Most People Make

The default plan for breaking a bad habit is to simply stop doing it. Stop snacking at night. Stop scrolling in bed. It feels direct, and it feels like it should work.

What's Actually Happening

Every habit runs on a loop: a cue that triggers it, the behavior itself, and a reward that closes the loop. Your brain doesn't judge whether that reward is good or bad for your goals, it just learns that a specific cue leads to a specific payoff, and over time the behavior becomes automatic. Simply removing the behavior leaves what's called a behavioral gap: the cue still fires, the old reward is still being chased, and there's nothing new filling that space. That gap is almost always filled by the same old habit unless something is deliberately put there instead.

How to Actually Engineer a New Habit

1
Identify the cue
Notice exactly what triggers the habit. Getting into bed, finishing dinner, a stressful moment at work.
2
Clarify the reward you're actually chasing
Stress relief, boredom relief, or simple pleasure. Name it specifically instead of assuming it's about the food or the phone itself.
3
Substitute the behavior, not the reward
Find a new behavior that meets the same need. Swap scrolling for a book if the reward was relaxation before sleep.
4
Reduce friction for the new habit
Engineer your environment so the new behavior is the easy option. Phone outside the room, book right next to the bed.
5
Reinforce through repetition
Consistency builds the habit, not perfection. Occasional slip-ups don't erase progress as long as the pattern continues.

The 5 Habits Worth Replacing This Year

1
Skipping breakfast
Skipping breakfast is linked to more hunger, more cravings, more snacking, and lower daily energy expenditure. Replace it with a simple 25-30g protein, 5-10g fiber breakfast: overnight oats with protein powder, pre-boiled eggs with fruit, or a protein smoothie. It takes under 5 minutes to put together.
2
Doom scrolling at night
Blue light delays melatonin release and lengthens time to fall asleep, and chronic poor sleep is tied to significantly higher risk of obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. Replace it with a low-stimulation wind-down: reading, an audiobook, stretching, or a warm shower, with your phone charging outside the bedroom.
3
Sitting for most of the day
A sedentary lifestyle significantly raises the risk of insulin resistance and heart disease. Replace it with a daily step goal of 8,000-10,000 steps, broken into 2-3 short walks, plus standing or walking during phone calls.
4
Avoiding strength training
Adults lose 3-8% of muscle mass per decade after 30 without resistance training, a major driver of long-term independence and mobility. Replace inaction with 2-3 full body sessions a week covering a push, a pull, a squat, and a hinge movement. Schedule it like a non-negotiable meeting.
5
Mindless late-night snacking
Late night snacking is rarely about hunger, and it can quietly add 500-600 untracked calories to a day. Replace boredom eating with a planned, satisfying evening snack if you're genuinely hungry, and address the boredom or stress directly with a non-food activity otherwise.

Key Takeaways
  • Every habit runs on a cue-behavior-reward loop. Removing a habit without replacing the behavior leaves a gap that gets refilled by the old pattern.
  • Skipping breakfast backfires: it increases hunger, cravings, and nighttime snacking rather than preventing them.
  • Chronic poor sleep from nighttime scrolling is tied to significantly higher risk of obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease.
  • Replacing an hour of sitting with an hour of exercise was linked to a 42% lower risk of dying within 6 years in highly sedentary people.
  • Strength training 2-3 times a week can slow or reverse age-related muscle loss, one of the strongest predictors of long-term independence.

None of these five habits require a complete personality overhaul. Each one has a specific cue, a specific reward, and a specific replacement that meets that same need without the long-term cost. Pick one, engineer your environment around the swap, and let repetition do the rest before moving to the next.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why can't I just stop a bad habit through willpower alone?

Removing a habit without replacing it leaves a behavioral gap. Your brain doesn't distinguish good habits from bad ones, it just completes the cue-behavior-reward loop it's used to, so the old behavior tends to return unless something new is deliberately substituted in its place.

Is skipping breakfast bad if I'm intermittent fasting?

Skipping breakfast specifically is linked to increased hunger, cravings, and snacking later in the day, along with lower daily energy expenditure. Any caloric restriction benefits of fasting come from eating fewer total calories, not from the timing itself, and most people manage hunger better with a protein and fiber forward breakfast.

How much does sitting all day actually affect long-term health?

Significantly. In highly sedentary people, replacing just one hour of sitting with one hour of exercise was linked to a 42% lower risk of dying within a 6-year period, and replacing an hour of sitting with light activity like walking or housework was linked to a 30% reduction.

How many days a week do I need to strength train to get the health benefits?

Two to three sessions a week is enough to start reducing age-related muscle loss and improving markers like insulin sensitivity and bone density. Two sessions a week is a reasonable starting point if that's all your schedule allows.
