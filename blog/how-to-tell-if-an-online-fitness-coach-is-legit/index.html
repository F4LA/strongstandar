Is Your Online Fitness Coach Actually Legit?

The Instagram looks impressive and the testimonials sound great, but you've been burned before and you're not sure what actually separates a real coach from someone good at marketing. Cody McBroom, who has coached online for 16 years, breaks down exactly what to check.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· July 27, 2026 · 9 min read
CM
Cody McBroom
Online fitness coach of 16 years, founder of a coaching business running for over eight years. Now mentors other coaches, and has thought deeply about what separates legitimate coaching from marketing hype.
Quick Answer

A legitimate coach's business runs on client results, reviews, and referrals, not on how polished their content is. Check three things before you sign up: does their philosophy match what you actually want, do they have a real track record in this specific business, and are they transparent about pricing, methods, and who will actually coach you.

You've scrolled past a dozen fitness accounts today. Every one of them has a jacked before-and-after, a caption about mindset, and a link in bio. Some of them are probably legitimate. Some of them are very good at marketing and not much else. You've maybe even been burned once already, paid for a program that felt generic, or worked with someone who disappeared the moment you had a real question.

I talked with Cody McBroom, who has coached online for 16 years, about this exact problem. His answer was refreshingly specific: this isn't a mystery you have to solve on gut feeling. There are three concrete things to check, and a short list of red flags that predict a bad experience almost every time.

Why Judging a Coach by Their Content Gets People Burned

The Assumption Most People Make

Most people evaluate an online coach the way they'd evaluate an influencer: production quality, follower count, how good the transformations look in the grid. It makes sense, because that's the only information visible from a phone screen.

What Actually Predicts a Good Experience

McBroom points out that the barrier to entry for calling yourself an online coach is close to zero. Anyone with an Instagram account and a decent physique can put "online coach" in their bio, whether or not they've ever actually delivered a result for a client. That's exactly why content quality is such a poor filter. The things that actually predict whether someone will deliver are invisible in a 15 second video, and they're the same three things McBroom tells his own mentees to look for when choosing a mentor themselves.

The Three Things Worth Actually Checking

1
Values alignment
Does their approach match what you actually want long term? A coach who preaches extreme, unsustainable methods will build you a plan around what they're willing to sacrifice, not what you are.
2
Real track record
Have they actually run a coaching business, or built a following and pivoted into selling advice about running one? Ask directly what they've done. Vague or evasive answers are the answer.
3
Transparency
Are pricing, methods, and who will actually coach you clearly stated? A coach confident in their value has nothing to hide about any of these.

None of these require you to become a detective. McBroom's own words: it's pretty easy to see who aligns with you and who doesn't once you know what to actually look for.

The Red Flags That Actually Predict a Bad Experience

Two specific patterns come up constantly in McBroom's world, and both are easy to spot once you know to look for them.

The first is fake scarcity. A post that says "looking for 5 people" sitting up for a month straight, never updated, is a script, not a real cap. The second, more serious one, is what McBroom calls shadow coaching: you sign up believing a specific coach will work with you directly, and instead a team member handles your calls under that coach's name. That's not the same as a coach being upfront about having a team. The problem isn't the team, it's the lie about who you're actually talking to.

Red Flags
  • "Looking for 5 spots" post that never changes or updates
  • No pricing listed anywhere, only "message me for info"
  • You're told you'll work with them, then handed off silently
  • Big following, but no verifiable client results or reviews
Good Signs
  • Clear, consistent pricing stated up front
  • Upfront about who coaches you day to day, team or not
  • Real, verifiable reviews tied to specific results
  • Willing to answer direct questions about their own track record
You could just be honest, and if you believe in what you're doing and you're good at it, people literally don't care that it's not you personally on every call.

What It Looks Like When a Coaching Business Is the Real Deal

McBroom's own coaching company doesn't lean on his personal following to grow. A client recently signed up and then called back to sign up her husband too, without ever knowing who McBroom was or listening to a single podcast episode. She found the company through a Google search and reviews. Hundreds of reviews, and by his account, not a single bad one.

That's the actual signal worth trusting: a business that keeps growing because of what it delivers, verifiable by strangers who've never met the coach in person, not because of how good the content looks in your feed.

Key Takeaways
  • Content quality and follower count are a poor filter, because the barrier to calling yourself a coach is nearly zero.
  • Check three things instead: values alignment, an actual track record, and transparency about pricing and who coaches you.
  • A "looking for 5 spots" post that never changes is a script, not a real cap. Treat it as a signal, not a coincidence.
  • Shadow coaching, being handed to a different person than advertised, is different from a coach being upfront about their team.
  • Real reviews from strangers who found a coach through search, not through their following, are one of the strongest signals available.

The question was never really whether a coach looks legitimate online. It's whether their business would still hold up if you asked the three direct questions above, out loud, and waited for a straight answer. The coaches worth working with will give you one without hesitation.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it a red flag if a coach doesn't list their prices online?

It can be. Coaches confident in the value they deliver are usually willing to be upfront about pricing. Random, constantly shifting promos with no listed rate are more often a sign of sales pressure than a sign of quality.

What is shadow coaching in online fitness?

Shadow coaching is when you sign up believing a specific person will coach you directly, but a different team member handles your calls and check-ins under that person's name. It's different from a coach being transparent about having a team; the issue is the deception, not the team itself.

Does a coach need a huge following to be legitimate?

No. Many legitimate coaching businesses grow primarily through client results, reviews, and referrals rather than social media reach. A smaller following paired with strong reviews and a clear track record is often a better sign than a large one with vague results.

What should I ask a coach before signing up?

Ask what results they've actually produced for people like you, how long they've been coaching in this specific format, who will be coaching you day to day, and what their pricing and cancellation terms are. Vague or evasive answers to any of these are worth taking seriously.
