Debunking Nutrition Misinformation

Your feed is full of confident claims about what's secretly ruining your health. Dr. Adrian Chavez breaks down two viral claims and the tactics behind why they spread so easily.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· September 2, 2026 · 7 min read
AC
Dr. Adrian Chavez
Nutrition scientist specializing in helping people separate accurate nutrition science from viral misinformation.
Quick Answer

No, labeling a food "natural" doesn't inherently make it healthier, and no, artificial sweeteners have not been shown to "break your DNA" in the way viral claims suggest. Both claims rely on emotionally charged, attention-grabbing language rather than accurate scientific evidence.

If you spend any time on social media, you've seen the confident, sensational claims: this food is secretly making you fat, this ingredient is destroying your health. Dr. Adrian Chavez, a nutrition scientist, breaks down two common viral claims and the tactics used to make them spread.

Claim One: Being Fat Is About Food Quality, Not Quantity

The Viral Claim

You're not fat because you eat too much food, you're fat because you eat the wrong foods.

Dr. Chavez notes there's a kernel of truth buried here, minimally processed animal and plant foods do tend to be more satiating, meaning your brain's natural fullness signals work better on them. But framing this as an absolute, "you are not fat because you eat too much," oversimplifies a genuinely more nuanced reality where both food quality and quantity matter.

Claim Two: Artificial Sweeteners Break Your DNA

The Viral Claim

Artificial sweeteners like sucralose and aspartame are "linked to breaking your DNA."

This kind of vague, scary-sounding phrase, "breaking your DNA," isn't backed by the evidence in the way it's presented, and it's not entirely clear the person making the claim can define what it would even mean scientifically. Dr. Chavez points out this is a common tactic: use a technical-sounding but vague phrase that sounds alarming without needing to explain or support it.

What does breaking your DNA mean? I don't really know, and I know what it actually means in the context of what he's talking about, probably absolutely nothing.

The Bigger Pattern: "Natural" Doesn't Mean Better

Both claims lean on the same underlying assumption: that natural is inherently good and man-made is inherently bad. This idea sounds intuitive, but it doesn't hold up. Plenty of naturally occurring substances in the environment can be harmful, while plenty of man-made innovations, from medicine to food safety technology, have significantly extended human lifespan.

Dr. Chavez points to a specific data point often distorted by this kind of content: while more people are overweight today than in previous eras, heart disease death rates have actually dropped by roughly 66% over the last 75 years, and cancer rates haven't increased the way some claims imply. People are living longer overall and dying less from infectious disease, which changes which health conditions show up more often in the data.

Key Takeaways
  • Whether a food is natural or man-made doesn't determine whether it's actually healthy or harmful.
  • Claims about "breaking your DNA" from artificial sweeteners use vague, alarming language without solid scientific backing.
  • Heart disease death rates have dropped significantly over the past 75 years, contradicting the narrative that modern life is making everyone sicker.
  • People living longer overall means more visible age-related disease, not necessarily worse overall health outcomes.
  • Absolute claims and fear-based, vague technical language are common tactics used to generate attention on social media.

Sensational claims are built to grab your attention in a few seconds, not to accurately reflect nuanced science. Learning to spot absolute statements and vague, scary-sounding phrases is one of the most useful skills for navigating nutrition content online.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is natural food always healthier than processed food?

No. Labeling something as natural doesn't inherently make it healthier, and labeling something man-made doesn't inherently make it worse. Many naturally occurring substances can be harmful, while many man-made innovations have significantly improved health and lifespan.

Do artificial sweeteners break your DNA?

This is a common viral claim without solid scientific support behind it in the way it's presented. Sensational phrases like this are a common tactic used to generate attention on social media rather than convey accurate scientific information.

Have chronic disease rates actually gotten worse over the last century?

Not across the board. Heart disease death rates have dropped significantly over the past 75 years, and cancer rates haven't increased in the dramatic way some claims suggest. People are living longer and experiencing more age-related disease simply because they're dying less from infectious disease.

How can you spot misleading nutrition content on social media?

Watch for absolute claims ("you are fat because of X"), fear-based language about vague harms like "breaking your DNA," and reflexive praise for anything labeled natural while condemning anything man-made, since these are common tactics used to grab attention rather than convey accurate information.
