Can You Be Healthy at a Higher Body Fat?

Your blood markers look fine, so does the extra weight even matter? Dr. Joey breaks down what fat tissue actually does inside your body, and why the answer isn't as simple as "healthy at any size."

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· August 7, 2026 · 9 min read
Quick Answer

By the standard of blood markers, yes, some people with higher body fat can be considered healthy. But body fat itself is an independent risk factor for worse health, so that same person would likely see their markers improve further by reducing it. The bigger risk many people miss is being "skinny fat": a normal weight with low muscle mass and high body fat, which carries its own negative health outcomes.

Your last blood panel came back fine. Cholesterol, blood sugar, blood pressure, all within range. So the extra 20 or 30 pounds you're carrying can't really be hurting you, right? That's the exact question a lot of people are asking, and the honest answer has more nuance than either side of the debate wants to admit.

I'm Dr. Joey Munoz, nutrition science PhD, and I want to walk through what body fat actually does inside your body, why it's not simply good or bad, and why muscle mass matters just as much as the number on the scale.

Fat Isn't Just Storage, It's an Active Organ

Fat tissue constantly releases molecules into your bloodstream. Some, like adiponectin, have anti-inflammatory properties. Others, like TNF-alpha and interleukin-6, can have pro-inflammatory effects. Under normal conditions, this system stays balanced. The ratio between the two is what matters, not whether one exists at all.

When you gain excess body fat, fat cells grow and multiply faster than your blood supply can keep up. That creates a low-oxygen environment inside the fat tissue, and low oxygen shifts what fat cells release: more inflammatory molecules, fewer anti-inflammatory ones. That shift is what leads to chronic low-grade inflammation, the kind that quietly disrupts insulin signaling, raises blood pressure, and stiffens your arteries over years.

Why "Healthy at Any Size" Isn't Quite True

The Claim
"You can be healthy at any size."

There's a real range where body fat doesn't meaningfully harm your health, and someone with excess fat who's active, eats well, and has strong blood markers can genuinely be healthier than someone at a "normal" weight who is sedentary and unhealthy. But that comparison isn't the same as saying body fat has zero cost. Independent of every other lifestyle factor, higher body fat is linked to worse cardiovascular, metabolic, and inflammatory markers. There's a point past which no amount of good habits fully offsets it.

The honest version is this: could someone with excess body fat be relatively healthy based on standard markers? Yes. Would that same person likely be even healthier if they reduced body fat while keeping their good habits? Also yes. Both statements are true at the same time, which is exactly why this topic gets oversimplified in both directions online.

The Bigger Blind Spot: Being "Skinny Fat"

Here's the part most people miss entirely. Being a normal weight doesn't automatically mean you're healthy. There's a recognized condition in research called normal weight obesity, or what most people call "skinny fat": someone whose weight and BMI look completely normal, but who carries a high body fat percentage and very little muscle mass.

This population shows negative health outcomes similar to people who are overweight, despite the number on the scale looking fine. It's not really about your weight at all. It's about the ratio of muscle to fat you're carrying.

It's not weight that predicts health. It's the ratio of muscle to fat you're carrying.

Why Muscle Mass Changes the Whole Equation

Muscle is the largest reservoir for glucose in your body. The more muscle you carry, the better your body handles blood sugar, both because muscle stores glucose as glycogen and because muscle contraction itself pulls glucose out of your blood without even needing insulin. That's a second, independent pathway for blood sugar control that low-muscle people simply don't have access to.

Normal Weight, Low Muscle
  • High body fat percentage despite normal BMI
  • Smaller glucose reservoir, worse blood sugar regulation
  • Higher risk of the same outcomes seen in obesity
Higher Weight, High Muscle
  • More muscle mass to buffer blood sugar
  • Better strength, bone density, and fall resilience with age
  • Often healthier markers than a sedentary "normal weight" person

This is also why muscle mass matters so much as you get older. Research on older adults found that low muscle size combined with low muscle function predicted a roughly twelvefold greater risk of losing independence later in life, things like needing help showering or getting out of bed. That's not a small effect from a "nice to have" trait. It's tied directly to whether your body composition supports you or works against you for decades.

So What Should You Actually Focus On?

Instead of fixating only on the scale or only on body fat percentage, the more useful question is whether you're carrying enough muscle relative to your fat. That's the variable that actually predicts how your body handles blood sugar, inflammation, and long-term physical resilience, far more than weight alone. This is different from the question of whether to prioritize fat loss or muscle first, since here we're talking about the health consequences of the ratio itself, not which one to train first.

Key Takeaways
  • Fat tissue is metabolically active. Excess fat shifts it toward releasing more inflammatory molecules and fewer anti-inflammatory ones.
  • "Healthy at any size" oversimplifies a real nuance: body fat is an independent risk factor even when other lifestyle factors are excellent.
  • Being a normal weight doesn't guarantee good health. Skinny fat, or normal weight obesity, carries its own negative outcomes.
  • Muscle mass improves blood sugar regulation through two separate pathways, one of which doesn't even require insulin.
  • Low muscle mass and function in older adults is linked to roughly a twelvefold greater risk of losing independence later in life.

The number on the scale was never the full story. What actually predicts your long-term health is the balance between how much fat you're carrying and how much muscle you have to support it. That's the variable worth paying attention to, at any age and at any size.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you be healthy with a higher body fat percentage?

By the standard of blood markers and how you feel, yes, some people with higher body fat are healthy. But body fat itself is an independent risk factor for worse health, meaning that same person would likely see their markers improve further if they reduced body fat.

What is skinny fat and why is it a health risk?

Skinny fat, or normal weight obesity, describes someone whose weight looks normal but who has a high body fat percentage and low muscle mass. Research shows this group has negative health outcomes similar to those seen in people who are overweight, despite having a normal BMI.

Why does excess body fat cause inflammation?

As fat cells grow, blood supply and oxygen delivery to the fat tissue decreases, creating a low oxygen environment. This triggers fat tissue to release more inflammatory molecules and fewer anti-inflammatory ones, leading to chronic low-grade inflammation that disrupts insulin signaling and cardiovascular health.

Is the phrase healthy at any size accurate?

Not entirely. There is a range where body fat does not meaningfully harm health, but beyond that range, body fat becomes an independent contributor to worse health outcomes regardless of how healthy someone's lifestyle otherwise is.
