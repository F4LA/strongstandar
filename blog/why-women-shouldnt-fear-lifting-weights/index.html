Strong, Not Skinny

You've been told lifting heavy will make you bulky, so you stick to cardio and eat as little as possible. Megan Evans shares how letting go of that fear changed far more than just her physique.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· September 9, 2026 · 7 min read
ME
Megan Evans
University student who worked with Dr. Joey for 9 months, shifting from cardio-focused training to consistent strength training.
Quick Answer

No, lifting heavy weights will not make women bulky. Megan Evans spent years avoiding weights out of that exact fear, defaulting to hours of cardio and under-eating instead. Nine months of consistent strength training later, she lifts heavily four to five times a week, eats more food, and no longer fears the scale, with zero unwanted bulk.

A common misconception keeps many young women away from the weight room entirely: the fear that lifting heavy will make them bulky. Megan Evans, a university student, lived that exact fear for years before working with Dr. Joey for 9 months to build a completely different relationship with training.

The Cardio-Only Cycle

Megan's story started during the pandemic: home workouts, heavy focus on cardio and trendy ab routines, and undereating. She lost weight compared to her first year of university, but fell into what she describes as a genuinely difficult headspace, feeling like she always needed to restrict food and always needed to do cardio, while being too scared to pick up weights.

I was so scared to pick up weights because I didn't want to get bulky. I didn't know how to train properly. It's easy to see online that you need to eat more and lift heavier, but I didn't know how to actually implement that in real practice.

The University Gym Divide

Megan describes a telling scene from her university gym: all the cardio machines upstairs, packed with women, and the weight room downstairs, dominated by men. This physical divide reflected a widespread misconception that cardio is the path to "fit and lean" while lifting is reserved for people trying to get big, a false dichotomy that keeps many women away from strength training entirely.

What Actually Changed Over 9 Months

Before

Hours of cardio, restrictive eating, fear of the scale, avoiding weights entirely out of fear of getting bulky.

After 9 Months

Lifting heavy 4-5 times a week, eating more food, no longer afraid of the scale, and genuinely enjoying training.

Megan didn't just change her physical results, she describes the mental shift as equally significant, changing how she views exercise, lifting, and nutrition as part of her overall lifestyle rather than a source of constant anxiety.

Reframing What Exercise Is For

Perhaps the most useful insight Megan shares is a reframe: viewing exercise not as a means to get smaller, but as a tool to improve your overall capabilities, physical and mental. She credits much of her success in personal relationships, professional life, and confidence to lessons learned through consistent strength training.

It's not just about getting smaller. Take this seriously and make it an integral component of your life, and you will not regret it a couple years down the road.

Her Advice for Someone Starting Out

Megan's advice for women hesitant to start: take a breath, and remember that nobody at the gym is paying attention to you the way you assume they are. Trust the process, even when the scale moves in a direction that feels uncomfortable at first, and give the discomfort of starting time to turn into something you genuinely look forward to.

Key Takeaways
  • Lifting heavy weights does not make women bulky, that requires years of dedicated training and a specific caloric surplus.
  • The belief that cardio is for "toning" and lifting is for "bulking" is a common but inaccurate misconception.
  • A 9-month shift to consistent strength training changed both Megan's physical results and her relationship with food and exercise.
  • Viewing exercise as a tool to build capability, rather than purely to shrink your body, supports a healthier long-term relationship with training.
  • The discomfort of starting strength training tends to fade as consistency builds genuine enjoyment and confidence.

If fear of getting "too big" or "too bulky" has kept you out of the weight room, Megan's experience is a reminder that this fear rarely matches reality, and the benefits of strength training extend well beyond how your body looks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will lifting heavy weights make women bulky?

No. This is one of the most common fears women have around strength training, but building a visibly "bulky" physique requires years of dedicated training and typically a calorie surplus, not simply lifting heavier weights consistently.

Why do many young women default to cardio instead of weightlifting?

A common misconception is that cardio is the path to getting "fit and lean" while lifting is for getting bulky. This belief often leads to years of cardio-focused routines paired with under-eating, rather than a more sustainable, muscle-building approach.

What's a healthier way to think about exercise than "getting smaller"?

Viewing exercise as a tool to improve your overall capabilities, physical and mental, rather than purely a means to shrink your body, tends to support a longer-lasting, more positive relationship with training and food.

Does strength training actually offer benefits beyond physical appearance?

Yes. Many people report that lessons learned through consistent strength training, discipline, patience, and confidence, meaningfully carry over into personal relationships, professional life, and overall mental resilience.
