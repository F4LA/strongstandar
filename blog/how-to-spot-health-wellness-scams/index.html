How to Spot a Wellness Scam

You've paid for programs, supplements, and protocols that promised to fix what was "uniquely wrong" with you. Dr. Joey breaks down the exact four-step playbook wellness scammers use, so you can spot it before you pay for it again.

Dr. Joey Munoz, PhD
· August 10, 2026 · 9 min read
Quick Answer

Most wellness scams follow the same four-step pattern: convince you that something is uniquely wrong with you, discredit real science so you can't verify their claims, use real or borrowed credentials to sound like an authority, then silence the people who had bad experiences while spotlighting the few who got lucky. Recognizing this pattern is the fastest way to protect yourself.

You've tried things before. Maybe it was a detox protocol, a supplement stack, or a "metabolic reset" that promised to finally fix what nothing else could. You spent real money, maybe thousands of dollars, and walked away with no results and the nagging feeling that you did something wrong.

I'm Dr. Joey Munoz, and I want to walk you through the exact four-step playbook that almost every wellness scam follows, using a real client story, so you can recognize it instantly the next time you see it.

The Client Who Paid Thousands for Nothing

Last week we signed up a new client at Strong Standard. She's 69, a nurse, well-educated, and had just finished a year of cancer treatment including a hysterectomy and months of radiation. She wanted to rebuild her health and get back into shape, which is a completely reasonable goal after everything she'd been through.

She did what most people do: she searched online and found someone who presented as a credible expert, also with real credentials. She paid thousands of dollars for their help. What she got back was a list of a hundred different supplements, liver cleanses, and detox protocols. What she didn't get was any actual results. No weight loss, no improvement in her health or body composition. Just an empty bank account and the same problems she started with.

She wasn't undisciplined. She wasn't uneducated. She got scammed by someone who knew exactly how to sound credible.

Step One: They Convince You Something Is Uniquely Wrong With You

Every one of these playbooks starts the same way: making you feel broken. Before they sell you anything, they need you to believe there's a specific, hidden reason you haven't succeeded, something ordinary advice can't fix.

The Claim

"Your metabolism is damaged, you have toxic buildup in your intestines, and we need a specific protocol to fix it before your body can lose weight."

Notice the pattern: it's always something you can't easily verify yourself, and it always requires their specific test or product to "diagnose." That's not a coincidence. It's the entire business model.

Step Two: They Discredit Real Science

Once you believe something is uniquely wrong with you, the next step is making you distrust the people who could actually tell you otherwise. This usually shows up as sweeping claims that doctors only treat symptoms, that mainstream medicine is hiding something, or that there are secrets your doctor doesn't want you to know.

Here's the thing: there's no such thing as "mainstream science" versus some other, more honest science. There's just science, meaning claims that have actually been tested and hold up. Discrediting it isn't a bold truth-telling move, it's a tactic that removes your ability to fact-check what they're telling you next.

Step Three: They Use Credentials and Scientific Language to Sound Like an Authority

This is the step that catches even well-educated people off guard, because a lot of these individuals genuinely do have some kind of scientific background. They're often skilled communicators too, sometimes more skilled than the actual researchers doing the real work in labs.

The tell is usually in the language. Terms get thrown around that sound impressive but explain very little: cellular senescence, hormonal cascades, metabolic damage. Strip away the jargon and there's often nothing underneath it, just a simple, testable claim dressed up to sound complicated.

Credentials do not immediately mean anything. There are so many people with credentials doing extremely crappy work.

Step Four: They Filter the Feedback

The final step is what keeps the whole cycle running. People who spend money and get no results are discouraged from speaking out, sometimes through legal threats, sometimes simply through shame. Meanwhile, the handful of people who happened to get lucky are showcased loudly as proof it works.

If a thousand people buy something, a few of them will improve no matter what, just by chance, or because they happened to also change other habits at the same time. Those few become the testimonials. The other 995 people go quiet.

What She Actually Needed

It took about 15 minutes of conversation to identify what our client actually needed, and none of it involved a mysterious hidden problem. She had a history of strength training but hadn't been consistent, she was on a GLP-1 medication and losing muscle mass quickly as a result, she wasn't eating enough protein, and she struggled with emotional eating at night and under stress.

None of that required a detox. It required a real strategy, some education about why these specific things mattered for her situation, and consistent support to actually follow through.

Key Takeaways
  • Be wary of anyone who tells you something uniquely wrong with you is the real reason nothing has worked.
  • Discrediting "mainstream science" is a red flag, not a sign of deeper insight. There's only science that's been tested or hasn't.
  • Credentials and confident, scientific-sounding language make someone persuasive, not automatically correct.
  • A handful of lucky results get spotlighted while the majority of failures get silenced or shamed into staying quiet.
  • What actually works is a strategy suited to your life, real education, and consistent accountability, not a secret protocol.

The next time someone tells you that you need a special test or protocol before you can start seeing results, ask yourself which step of this playbook you're standing in. Real progress rarely needs a hidden diagnosis. It needs a strategy you can actually follow.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the first sign of a wellness scam?

The first sign is being told there's something uniquely wrong with you, like toxic buildup, a damaged metabolism, or hormone imbalance, that a special test or protocol needs to identify and fix before you can see results. Real experts start with your actual habits and behaviors, not a mysterious hidden problem.

Do credentials guarantee someone is a legitimate health expert?

No. Many people selling unproven protocols and supplements have real degrees and credentials, and use them specifically to build credibility and sell products. Credentials make someone more persuasive, not automatically more correct.

Why do wellness scams feel so convincing?

They work because they combine a kernel of truth, such as having tried and failed at something before, with sensational claims that discredit real science and position the seller as the only one who has the answer. The combination of real frustration and confident, scientific-sounding language builds trust quickly.

What should you actually look for in health and fitness coaching?

Look for a strategy that fits your actual goals and lifestyle, real education so you understand why you're doing what you're doing, and consistent accountability and support. Those three things, not a secret protocol, are what actually produce lasting results.
